## 1. Business model foundations (including the course framework)

**BM01. Teece, D. J. (2010). Business models, business strategy and innovation. *Long Range Planning, 43*(2-3), 172-194. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lrp.2009.07.003**
[V-open: Crossref; abstract via Semantic Scholar]. A business model is the design of the mechanisms by which a firm creates, delivers and captures value. It is management's hypothesis about what customers want and how the firm gets paid, and it gives an advantage only if it is hard to imitate. *Use:* anchor definition in 2.1. WHAT and HOW map to creation and delivery, ECONOMIC MODEL to capture.

**BM02. Zott, C., Amit, R., & Massa, L. (2011). The business model: Recent developments and future research. *Journal of Management, 37*(4), 1019-1042. https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206311406265**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. A review that finds no agreed definition and a literature in silos. Common themes: the BM as a unit of analysis, a system-level (activity system) view, and value creation as well as capture. *Use:* optional support for taking the BM of the focal product as the unit of analysis.

**BM03. Osterwalder, A., & Pigneur, Y. (2010). *Business model generation: A handbook for visionaries, game changers, and challengers*. Wiley.**
[V-open: Open Library, ISBN 9780470876411, 13 July 2010]. The nine-block Business Model Canvas [M content]. *Use:* bridge from WHO/WHAT/HOW/EM to the Canvas used in the course slides (L06). It is not peer reviewed.

## 2. Business model innovation and adaptation

**BMI01. Foss, N. J., & Saebi, T. (2017). Fifteen years of research on business model innovation: How far have we come, and where should we go? *Journal of Management, 43*(1), 200-227. https://doi.org/10.1177/0149206316675927**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. A systematic review of 150 BMI articles (2000-2015). The field has problems of construct clarity and gaps on antecedents, contingencies and outcomes. *Use:* the definition of BMI behind the D0/D1/D2 threshold. Quote the definition from the text. The Crossref title omits the subtitle.

**BMI02. Saebi, T., Lien, L., & Foss, N. J. (2017). What drives business model adaptation? The impact of opportunities, threats and strategic orientation. *Long Range Planning, 50*(5), 567-581. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lrp.2016.06.006**
[V-open: Crossref; abstract not retrieved]. [M]: survey evidence that perceived threats drive BM adaptation more than opportunities, moderated by strategic orientation. *Use:* frames incumbents facing lower entry barriers as a case of BM adaptation under threat. This fits the supervisor's defensive framing. Read the findings before stating the direction of the effects.

**BMI03. Chesbrough, H. (2010). Business model innovation: Opportunities and barriers. *Long Range Planning, 43*(2-3), 354-363. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lrp.2009.07.010**
[V-open: Crossref]. [M]: barriers to BMI come from conflict with existing assets and models and from managerial cognition. Firms overcome them through experimentation. *Use:* explains research previews and betas (S1), and why acquired AI-natives are kept in parallel (S7).

**BMI04. Christensen, C. M. (1997). *The innovator's dilemma: When new technologies cause great firms to fail*. Harvard Business School Press.**
[V-open: Open Library]. [M]: disruptive technologies that start out inferior overtake incumbents because incumbents allocate resources to their existing customers. *Use:* frames AI-native entrants (Gamma) and general assistants. The cases show early incumbent responses, which is a useful contrast.

**BMI05. O'Reilly, C. A., III, & Tushman, M. L. (2004). The ambidextrous organization. *Harvard Business Review, 82*(4), 74-81.**
[V-open: hbr.org page for the April 2004 issue and the authors; volume and pages M]. Firms keep exploratory units separate from exploitative ones while integrating them at the top. *Use:* acquisitions kept in parallel or integrated (S7).

**BMI06. O'Reilly, C. A., III, & Tushman, M. L. (2013). Organizational ambidexterity: Past, present, and future. *Academy of Management Perspectives, 27*(4), 324-338. https://doi.org/10.5465/amp.2013.0025**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. A review of explore/exploit research. *Use:* the peer-reviewed anchor for BMI05.

## 3. AI and business model innovation

**AI01. Kanbach, D. K., Heiduk, L., Blueher, G., Schreiter, M., & Lahmann, A. (2024). The GenAI is out of the bottle: Generative artificial intelligence from a business model innovation perspective. *Review of Managerial Science, 18*(4), 1189-1220. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11846-023-00696-z**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract; five authors confirmed]. A scoping review of 513 data points that yields six propositions on how GenAI affects business models, with examples from software engineering, healthcare and finance. *Use:* the state of research for the generative phase.

**AI02. Jorzik, P., Klein, S. P., Kanbach, D. K., & Kraus, S. (2024). AI-driven business model innovation: A systematic review and research agenda. *Journal of Business Research, 182*, Article 114764. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2024.114764**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. A review of 180 articles. The field is fragmented and tends to treat BMI as a by-product of technology. It separates static and dynamic views of BMI. *Use:* positions the thesis and supports a dynamic (t0, t1, t2) view.

**AI03. Teng, D., Ye, C., & Martinez, V. (2025). Gen-AI's effects on new value propositions in business model innovation: Evidence from information technology industry. *Technovation, 143*, Article 103191. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.technovation.2025.103191**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. Based on 32 interviews plus archival data. It identifies five GenAI value-proposition approaches: knowledge-querying cloud solutions, content creation, AI agents, foundation models and upstream infrastructure. These are mapped to radical and incremental BMI. *Use:* the closest peer-reviewed study. The thesis tests "AI agents / content creation as value-proposition change" in creation-oriented SaaS incumbents.

**AI04. Åström, J., Reim, W., & Parida, V. (2022). Value creation and value capture for AI business model innovation: A three-phase process framework. *Review of Managerial Science, 16*(7), 2111-2133. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11846-022-00521-z**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. A single case of an AI provider: identify the prerequisites for value creation, match value-capture mechanisms, develop the offer. *Use:* value-capture vocabulary for credits and AI bundles (S5).

**AI05. Heimburg, V., Schreieck, M., & Wiesche, M. (2025). Complementor value co-creation in generative AI platform ecosystems. *Journal of Management Information Systems, 42*(2), 491-528. https://doi.org/10.1080/07421222.2025.2487310**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. OpenAI-ecosystem complementors handle GenAI's open-endedness and inscrutability through four mechanisms (system instructions, context data, curating inputs, revising outputs) and two logics (reap, differentiation). *Use:* theory for Canva, Adobe and Figma as complementors of AI assistants (S2). "Providing context data" matches S4. The gap to state: the study covers GPT builders, not incumbents.

**AI06. Saltan, A., & Smolander, K. (2021). Bridging the state-of-the-art and the state-of-the-practice of SaaS pricing: A multivocal literature review. *Information and Software Technology, 133*, Article 106510. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.infsof.2021.106510**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. A review of 387 items (57 white, 330 grey). SaaS pricing is under-managed, and the paper builds a taxonomy of pricing aspects, factors and challenges. *Use:* the pre-GenAI baseline for S5 and a precedent for using grey literature.

**AI07. Lehmann, S., & Buxmann, P. (2009). Pricing strategies of software vendors. *Business & Information Systems Engineering, 1*(6), 452-462. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12599-009-0075-y**
[V-open: Crossref; TLDR via Semantic Scholar]. Six pricing parameters: price formation, payment structure, assessment base, price discrimination, bundling, dynamic pricing. *Use:* sub-codes for the ECONOMIC MODEL row (seat vs credit vs usage as the assessment base).

**AI08. Azabagic, N., Gemser, G., & Giesen, E. (2026). The human side of generative artificial intelligence in business model innovation. *California Management Review, 68*(4), 130-154. https://doi.org/10.1177/00081256261445458**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. Strategy consultants use GenAI in BMI through "reflexive augmentation". *Use:* gap evidence only. This kind of research treats AI as a tool for designing BMs, not as a shock to software vendors.

**AI09. Najmaei, A., & Sadeghinejad, Z. (2026). From instrument to economic actor: Generative and agentic AI as a new logic of business model innovation. *Big Data and Cognitive Computing, 10*(11), Article 360. https://doi.org/10.3390/bdcc10110360**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. A concept paper with three logics: supporting (AI as instrument), generative (co-creator) and agentic (delegated economic actor). "Delegated reconfiguration authority" is the condition under which agentic action counts as BMI. *Use:* conceptual support for separating generative from agentic in BM terms. It is an MDPI journal, so cite with caution.

**AI10. Pettersson, M. O., Björkdahl, J., & Holgersson, M. (2025). Profiting from AI: Evidence from Ericsson's pursuit to capture value. *California Management Review, 67*(4), 5-20. https://doi.org/10.1177/00081256251349140**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. AI strategies split into bottom-line efficiency and top-line new businesses. The second is harder because it needs co-specialized complementary assets. *Use:* a recent application of Teece's lens to AI, bridging LENS01 and the cases.

**AI11. Bohnsack, R., & de Wet, M. (2025). *AI is the strategy: From agentic AI to autonomous business models onto strategy in the age of AI* (arXiv:2506.17339). arXiv.**
[V-open: arXiv API]. Introduces "Autonomous Business Models", in which agentic AI executes value creation, delivery and capture. *Use:* shows that agentic BM theorizing so far exists only as preprints.

## 4. Agentic AI: definitions and agency levels

**AG01. Sapkota, R., Roumeliotis, K. I., & Karkee, M. (2026). AI agents vs. agentic AI: A conceptual taxonomy, applications and challenges. *Information Fusion, 126*, Article 103599. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.inffus.2025.103599**
[V-open: Crossref and arXiv 2505.10468, with matching DOI]. AI agents are modular LLM systems that automate specific tasks through tools and prompts. Agentic AI adds multi-agent collaboration, dynamic task decomposition, persistent memory and coordinated autonomy. *Use:* the definitions in 3.1 and support for the 0-3 scale.

**AG02. Abou Ali, M., Dornaika, F., & Charafeddine, J. (2026). Agentic AI: A comprehensive survey of architectures, applications, and future directions. *Artificial Intelligence Review, 59*(1), Article 11. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-025-11422-4**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. A PRISMA review of 90 studies. It separates symbolic/classical agents from neural/generative ones and warns against "conceptual retrofitting". *Use:* a technical definition. The agents in the cases belong to the neural/generative lineage.

**AG03. Kumar, N., Wei, X., & Zhang, H. (2026). Agentic artificial intelligence as a new frontier in information systems: Promise, peril, and research opportunities. *Information & Management, 63*(3), Article 104317. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.im.2026.104317**
[V-open: Crossref for the metadata; abstract not retrievable, ScienceDirect returned 403]. Content is known only from earlier snippets. Read it before citing any claim.

**AG04. Li, M. M., Reinhard, P., & Leimeister, J. M. (2026). Agentic AI. *Business & Information Systems Engineering, 68*(5), 1191-1204. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12599-026-01009-w**
[V-open: Crossref; content not read]. A BISE catchword article and a new find. *Use:* a business-oriented definition from a top IS outlet, to compare with AG01.

**AG05. Parasuraman, R., Sheridan, T. B., & Wickens, C. D. (2000). A model for types and levels of human interaction with automation. *IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics - Part A: Systems and Humans, 30*(3), 286-297. https://doi.org/10.1109/3468.844354**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. Automation applies to four functions (information acquisition, analysis, decision selection, action implementation), each on a continuum from manual to automatic. *Use:* the theoretical anchor for the agency scale. Agents automate decision selection and action implementation. The 10-level scale usually attributed to this paper comes from Sheridan's earlier work, so check before citing levels.

## 5. Theoretical lenses

**LENS01. Teece, D. J. (1986). Profiting from technological innovation: Implications for integration, collaboration, licensing and public policy. *Research Policy, 15*(6), 285-305. https://doi.org/10.1016/0048-7333(86)90027-2**
[V-open: Crossref; content M]. Appropriability regime, dominant design and complementary assets (generic, specialized, co-specialized) decide who profits from an innovation. *Use:* the main lens requested by the supervisor. When GenAI makes the core capability imitable, incumbents defend built value through complementary assets: users, templates, brand kits, design systems, distribution. This supports S2, S4, S6 and S7.

**LENS02. Teece, D. J. (2018). Profiting from innovation in the digital economy: Enabling technologies, standards, and licensing models in the wireless world. *Research Policy, 47*(8), 1367-1387. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.respol.2017.01.015**
[V-open: Crossref; abstract via S2]. In the digital economy, value capture requires understanding platforms and ecosystems. *Use:* extends LENS01 to AI assistants and MCP ecosystems.

**LENS03. Tripsas, M. (1997). Unraveling the process of creative destruction: Complementary assets and incumbent survival in the typesetter industry. *Strategic Management Journal, 18*(S1), 119-142. https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1097-0266(199707)18:1+<119::AID-SMJ921>3.0.CO;2-0**
[V-open: Crossref; content M]. Typesetter incumbents with specialized complementary assets, especially font libraries, survived radical technological change. *Use:* a historical analog from a design industry: font libraries then, templates and brand kits now (S4, S6). This is a new suggestion; read it first.

**LENS04. Gawer, A., & Cusumano, M. A. (2014). Industry platforms and ecosystem innovation. *Journal of Product Innovation Management, 31*(3), 417-433. https://doi.org/10.1111/jpim.12105**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. Distinguishes internal (product) platforms from external (industry) platforms. *Use:* HOST (S3) and the role of complementor to AI assistants (S2).

**LENS05. Jacobides, M. G., Cennamo, C., & Gawer, A. (2018). Towards a theory of ecosystems. *Strategic Management Journal, 39*(8), 2255-2276. https://doi.org/10.1002/smj.2904**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. Modularity and non-generic complementarities explain why ecosystems emerge. *Use:* optional, for MCP and apps as modular interfaces.

**LENS06. Ceccagnoli, M., Forman, C., Huang, P., & Wu, D. J. (2012). Cocreation of value in a platform ecosystem: The case of enterprise software. *MIS Quarterly, 36*(1), 263-290. https://doi.org/10.2307/41410417**
[V-open: Crossref, abstract]. Small software vendors that join a major platform (SAP) gain in sales and IPO likelihood, conditional on appropriability. *Use:* a software precedent for joining another firm's platform (S2).

**LENS07. Shapiro, C., & Varian, H. R. (1999). *Information rules: A strategic guide to the network economy*. Harvard Business School Press.**
[V-open: Open Library, ISBN 087584863X, listed as 1998; the 1999 date is confirmed by the AMR review DOI 10.5465/amr.2000.3312931]. [M]: versioning, lock-in, switching costs, network effects, zero marginal cost. *Use:* pre-AI SaaS economics and why inference costs break them (S5).

**LENS08. Azoulay, P., Krieger, J., & Nagaraj, A. (2025). Old moats for new models: Openness, control, and competition in generative artificial intelligence. *Entrepreneurship and Innovation Policy and the Economy, 4*, 7-46. https://doi.org/10.1086/732852**
[V-open: Crossref; content V-snippet]. IP and secrecy will not prevent entry into GenAI, while control of complementary assets such as compute concentrates the market. *Use:* lower entry barriers plus complementary assets, one layer up the stack.

**NEW01. Mayer, A.-S., Kostis, A., Strich, F., & Holmström, J. (2025). Shifting dynamics: How generative AI as a boundary resource reshapes digital platform governance. *Journal of Management Information Systems, 42*(2), 400-430. https://doi.org/10.1080/07421222.2025.2487312**
[V-open: Crossref; abstract via S2]. GenAI tools integrated as boundary resources force new governance mechanisms and shift power between platform owner and complementors. *Use:* the theoretical home for the rival hypothesis "governance as a strategic lever" and for S3.

**NEW02. Hydari, M. Z., & Muzaffar, F. (2026). *Going headless? On the boundaries of vertical AI firms* (arXiv:2605.17812). arXiv.**
[V-open: arXiv API]. Ceding the interface to general agents ("going headless") is right for some firms and destroys value capture for others, through architectural choices that look like interface decisions. *Use:* the closest conceptual match to S2 and to "what incumbents retain". It concerns vertical software and is not peer reviewed.

**NEW03. Hydari, M. Z., & Muzaffar, F. (2026). *Redrawing the AI map: A theory of accountability boundaries in agentic ecosystems* (arXiv:2605.23179). arXiv.**
[V-open: arXiv API]. "Accountability assets" are complementary assets that make AI output legitimate and auditable. *Use:* governance rival hypothesis and S4.

### What exists and what does not (statement for 3.3 and the introduction)

- **No peer-reviewed study** was found on how creation-oriented SaaS incumbents (or SaaS firms in general) change their business model in response to *agentic* AI. The closest peer-reviewed works stop short in one way or another:
  - Teng et al. (2025) cover the generative phase in the IT industry, from the side of AI providers.
  - Heimburg et al. (2025) and Mayer et al. (2025) study GenAI platforms, but their complementors are small GPT builders and an education platform, not incumbents.
  - Najmaei & Sadeghinejad (2026) is conceptual and in an MDPI journal.
  - Li et al. (2026) only define the term.
- **Agentic AI and the SaaS business model** is covered only by preprints: Klotz (2026), Bohnsack & de Wet (2025), Hydari & Muzaffar (2026). The rest is analysts and consultants (Gartner, Deloitte, Bain, IDC).
- **AI pricing in SaaS** has no empirical peer-reviewed study after Saltan & Smolander (2021). What exists:
  - analytical models (Mahmood 2024; Ning et al. 2026; Li et al. 2026);
  - an SSRN framework for software services (Podolski 2026);
  - industry surveys.
- **Machine customers**: a practitioner column (Smith 2024) and Gartner; the agentic-commerce preprints are technical.
- **MCP**: about 130 arXiv preprints, all on security or measurement, none on vendor strategy.
- **Entry barriers**: emerging preprint evidence that GenAI raised entry, especially by solo founders (Kim et al. 2026; Cao & Bhatia 2025). Azoulay et al. (2025) argue that complementary assets decide who wins at the model layer. Nothing covers incumbents in creative or design software.
- **Creative and design software**: GenAI research addresses creators, labour and UX, not vendors' business models.
- **Limits of this search.** It relied on free APIs. OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar search were unavailable. Crossref ranking is fuzzy, and 2026 items may be indexed late. This is evidence of absence from a structured but non-exhaustive search, not a systematic review. **Re-run the strings below on Scopus and WoS before writing "no study exists".**
