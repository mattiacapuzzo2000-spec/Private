## 1. Research question

AI agents can now make graphics, decks and documents. They do it inside creation tools, and also from outside: ChatGPT, for example, can open Canva and make a poster for the user. At the same time AI made it much cheaper to build a creation tool, so new entrants like Gamma, and even the AI labs themselves (Claude Design, Google Pics), now compete with the incumbents. So the question is the one you raised in our call. How do incumbents defend what they built (their complementary assets, in Teece's 1986 sense) and change their business model?

*How are creation-oriented productivity SaaS incumbents evolving their business models as agentic AI enables digital artifacts to be produced by AI agents, both inside their products and through external agents that use them?*

It has 3 sub-questions. 1) Which roles do they take toward agents (own agent, tool for external agents, host of other firms' agents), and on which terms? 2) What changes in who, what, how and economics, and what stays the same? 3) How do they capture value and pay for the AI when an agent does the work?

Why it is not obvious. Bain tells incumbents, for some workflows, to "limit access to critical end points" (Crawford et al., 2025, p. 24). IDC says the opposite: "if an agent cannot find your capability, your capability does not exist" (Newmark, 2026). All 4 of my cases opened to external agents between 2024 and 2026. So the real question is on which terms they opened, and what they kept for themselves.

## 2. Scope, framework and method

- Population: cloud software (SaaS, in the NIST sense; Mell & Grance, 2011) whose main job is to create or edit a digital artifact (a graphic, a deck, a document, a design, a text), sold across industries, with a business model already in place in 2022. Out: task tools (Trello, monday.com), CRMs, communication tools and suites like Microsoft 365.
- Framework: the entrepreneurial formula from the course (who, what, how, plus the economics; Parolini, 2021). An AI feature that changes 1 component is a product change. Linked changes in 2 or more components, which need a new fit, are a business model change (Foss & Saebi, 2017).
- Method: a multiple-case study on public documents (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 2018): filings, earnings calls, pricing and help pages, announcements. I compare 3 moments: mid-2022, December 2024 and 30 November 2026. No interviews planned.

## 4. The cases

4 incumbents and 1 AI-native benchmark. For each case I have a dated register of AI moves with sources.

Figma (interface design). Founded in 2012, it launched its design tool in the browser in 2016. In 2022 it sold paid editor seats to product teams, on a freemium model with gross margins around 90%. Adobe agreed to buy it for USD 20 billion in 2022 and dropped the deal in 2023; Figma listed in 2025. With AI it opened its canvas to external agents (2025-2026), but writing needs a paid seat and will be metered. It enforced AI credit limits on every seat in March 2026 and made its own agent available to all, on credits, in October 2026. The cost shows in the filings: non-GAAP gross margin went from 92% in 2024 to 82% in Q3 2026, mostly because of AI infrastructure (Figma, 2026b).

Canva (graphics, presentations). Founded in Australia in 2012, it grew by making design easy for non-designers, with templates and a freemium model (Pro per person, Teams at a flat price). With AI it built its own agent (Canva AI 2.0, a research preview since April 2026) and its own models. It was also the first design connector inside ChatGPT (June 2025), then Claude. The cost of AI hit hard. In August 2026 Canva cut its 2026 growth target from 30% to 20% "to get the economics right" (Fortune, 2026). It raised Pro from USD 144 to 180 a year and went back 6 weeks later, and it cut free AI from 200 to 20 uses. It is also moving into marketing.

Adobe Express (social graphics, short videos). Adobe's template app, born from Adobe Spark and renamed in 2022. It is the challenger to Canva, with Premium at USD 9.99 a month since 2021. It went outside first: inside ChatGPT already in 2024, then Copilot, Claude, Slack and Gemini, with free entry and paid generation through Firefly credits. Its own assistant is still in beta. In June 2026 Adobe said it accepts less short-term ARR to grow free users, and that making money from agents "is basically about credit consumption" (Adobe, 2026).

Grammarly (text). Started in 2009 as a grammar checker that works on top of other apps, sold as freemium subscriptions. Since 2023 it changed more than any other case: GrammarlyGO with prompt limits (2023), the closure of its developer SDK (2024), the purchase of Coda and Superhuman, USD 1 billion of revenue-based financing, and in October 2025 the rename of the whole company to Superhuman, with an agent store. So its strategy is different. It hosts other firms' agents and builds a suite, while Grammarly itself has no connector for ChatGPT or Claude. The price did not move (USD 144 a year).

Gamma (benchmark). Launched in 2022, it reached USD 100 million of ARR and 70 million users by November 2025 with about 50 employees, according to its founder (TechCrunch, 2025). It charges more and more for the agent's work, not for generation, and it now competes with Canva and Adobe in marketing graphics.

## 5. First observations

- Opening to agents is universal, the terms are not: free entry (Adobe Express), paid plans only (Canva), paid seat and future metering (Figma), suite products only (Grammarly).
- The economics changed most, and cost drives it. Credits cap use, the seat stays the anchor, and I found no outcome-based pricing.
- Own agents arrive late (2026). So for now the agentic phase shows up in moves and terms more than in results.
