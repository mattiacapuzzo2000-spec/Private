## B. Brief history (about 220 words)

Grammarly was founded in 2009 by three Ukrainian entrepreneurs, Max Lytvyn, Alex Shevchenko and Dmytro Lider, and started as a grammar checker for students (Grammarly About page, 2026). It moved its headquarters to San Francisco while keeping engineering in Kyiv. Its defining choice was distribution: instead of building a word processor, it built an overlay that works "wherever you write", through browser extensions, desktop apps, mobile keyboards and Office and Google Docs add-ins, reaching 500,000 apps and 30 million daily users by November 2021, when it raised over USD 200 M at a USD 13 B valuation while already profitable (Grammarly blog, 17 Nov 2021; TechCrunch, 29 May 2025). In 2021-2022 it also opened a developer channel, the Text Editor SDK.

After ChatGPT, Grammarly added generative features (GrammarlyGO, March 2023), closed the SDK (announced July 2023, effective January 2024) and consolidated its plans into Grammarly Pro (2024). In December 2024 it agreed to acquire Coda, whose CEO Shishir Mehrotra became Grammarly's CEO. In 2025 it raised USD 1 B of revenue-based financing from General Catalyst, bought the email client Superhuman, launched writing "agents" (August) and, on 29 October 2025, renamed the company Superhuman, launching a four-product suite and the cross-app assistant Superhuman Go. In 2026 it bought Rows, GPTZero and Fathom, turned Coda into Superhuman Docs with an MCP server, and moved Grammarly Enterprise "through Superhuman Go".

## F. Draft section for the setup document (English, about 950 words)

### Grammarly (Superhuman)

**History in brief.** Grammarly was founded in 2009 by Max Lytvyn, Alex Shevchenko and Dmytro Lider, three Ukrainian entrepreneurs, as a grammar checker for students (Grammarly, About page, 2026). It later moved its headquarters to San Francisco and kept a large engineering base in Kyiv. Its distinctive choice was to work on top of other applications rather than build its own editor: by November 2021 it served 30 million people every day across more than 500,000 apps and websites, was profitable and cash-flow positive, and raised over USD 200 million at a USD 13 billion valuation (Grammarly, 17 Nov 2021; TechCrunch, 29 May 2025). Since 2023 the company has changed more than any other case in this study. In December 2024 it agreed to acquire the document platform Coda, whose co-founder Shishir Mehrotra became CEO (Grammarly, 17 Dec 2024). In 2025 it raised USD 1 billion of revenue-based financing from General Catalyst, bought the email client Superhuman and, on 29 October 2025, renamed the whole company Superhuman, with Grammarly becoming one product in a four-product suite (Grammarly, 29 May 2025; 1 Jul 2025; 29 Oct 2025).

**Business model before AI (2022).** *WHO:* English-language writers, from students to professionals and non-native speakers, reached through a large free tier; teams through Grammarly Business; schools through Grammarly for Education; and, from 2021, developers through a Text Editor SDK (Grammarly, 17 Nov 2021; 29 Sep 2022). *WHAT:* real-time, explainable feedback on correctness, clarity, tone and consistency, inside the user's own applications, with brand and style controls for teams. *HOW:* proprietary NLP and machine-learning models and a distribution infrastructure of browser extensions, desktop apps, mobile keyboards and integrations that place Grammarly in more than 500,000 apps. Grammarly transforms the text but does not host it: the email stays in Gmail, the document in Word. *ECONOMIC MODEL:* freemium subscription, with an individual Premium plan, per-seat Business plans and institutional contracts; "we make money from subscriptions, not from selling data or ads" (Grammarly, 17 Nov 2021). Before large language models the marginal cost of a suggestion was low, so the model was highly scalable. 2022 price points must still be checked on archived pricing pages.

**AI moves.** In the *generative phase* Grammarly added GrammarlyGO (March 2023), which composes, rewrites and drafts replies in any app, and immediately rationed it with monthly prompt caps (100 prompts for Free users, 500 for Premium at launch) (Grammarly, 9 Mar 2023; 9to5Google, 27 Apr 2023). In July 2023 it announced the closure of its developer SDK, effective January 2024, to focus on the core product, AI and enterprise customers (TechCrunch, 13 Jul 2023). In 2024 it merged Premium and Business into a single Pro plan at USD 12 per member per month billed annually, with 2,000 prompts, and launched Authorship, a tool that shows which parts of a text were written by a person or by AI (Grammarly support, 2026; Grammarly, 10 Mar 2026).
In the *agentic phase* the moves accelerated. In August 2025 Grammarly launched specialized "agents" (Proofreader, Reader Reactions, AI Grader, Citation Finder, AI Detector and others) in a new writing surface, at no extra cost (Grammarly, 18 Aug 2025). At the October 2025 rebrand it launched Superhuman Go, a proactive assistant built into the Grammarly extension that coordinates first- and third-party agents across apps, an Agent Store with partner agents, and an Agents SDK in closed beta (Grammarly, 29 Oct 2025). Partners such as Box, Gamma and Wayground followed in February 2026 (Superhuman, 23 Feb 2026). In 2026 the company bought Rows, GPTZero and Fathom, turned Coda into Superhuman Docs with an MCP server that lets ChatGPT and Claude read and write documents on paid plans, and released Go 1.0, where users build custom agents that run on schedules or events (Superhuman, 23 Feb, 23 Jun, 8 Jul, 12 Aug and 14 Sep 2026). In May 2026 Grammarly Enterprise started to be sold only "through Superhuman Go" (Grammarly enterprise page, 2026). One AI feature, "Expert Review", which attributed AI feedback to real writers without consent, was withdrawn after a class action (TechCrunch, 12 Mar 2026).

**Strategies identified (first version).** Grammarly's profile differs from the other cases. Its strongest strategies are *hosting third-party agents* (strategy 3), *expanding scope* into a work suite (6) and *acquisitions* (7), supported by an *own agent* whose autonomy grew from single-step generation to triggered custom agents (1). These choices share one logic: the asset that general-purpose assistants cannot easily copy is not Grammarly's text generation but its position at the user's cursor in a million applications, and the company turns that overlay into a platform on which its own agents, partner agents and acquired products run. This fits the complementary-assets lens (Teece, 1986): the incumbent defends what it built, distribution and daily habit, rather than the feature that AI commoditized. The financing structure matters too: capital repaid through a share of revenue, raised explicitly for sales, marketing and acquisitions, favors bundles and upsell. *Being used by external agents* (2) is weak for Grammarly itself: no official Grammarly connector for ChatGPT or Claude was found, and the SDK was closed. Opening happens only for the suite products that host artifacts (Docs, Mail), on paid plans. *Cost-driven monetization* (5) is present in a light form: the seat price has not moved, while AI use is capped by prompts, soft limits and announced usage-based pricing for agents (Superhuman, 12 Aug 2026). *Proprietary context* (4) is present through style guides, brand tones and acquired context sources (documents, email, meetings). Outcome-based pricing is absent. Governance, through Authorship, AI detection and the GPTZero acquisition, appears to be a deliberate lever, especially in education. What has not changed is also telling: Grammarly is still sold on its own, with the same free tier and the same USD 144 annual price as Premium in 2022.
