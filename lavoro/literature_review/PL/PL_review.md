## 2. Thematic synthesis

### 2.1 What a platform is, and where the incumbents sit

The foundational literature gives three vocabularies. Gawer and Cusumano (2014) separate internal platforms (a set of assets from which a firm derives products) from external or industry platforms that "act as a foundation upon which external innovators" build. Jacobides, Cennamo and Gawer (2018) add that ecosystems emerge where modularity lets interdependent firms coordinate without hierarchy and where complementarities are non-generic (unique or supermodular, one-way or two-way). Adner (2017) defines the ecosystem by its activity structure and alignment. Rietveld and Schilling (2021), in a review of 333 papers, show that platform competition was studied before 2020 almost entirely in transaction, video game, mobile and software markets. There was no AI-agent stream.

The three vocabularies give a first diagnostic for the thesis. In the S2 relationship (being used by an external agent), the creation incumbent is a complementor on somebody else's external platform: ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot, Gemini. In S3 (hosting third-party agents), the incumbent tries to be the platform owner for a small ecosystem of agents. The same firm can be both at once, which is the case for Grammarly/Superhuman (host through the Agent Store, complementor through the Docs MCP) and, in weaker form, for Adobe (host of add-ons, complementor of ChatGPT). The literature on dual positions is thin; Adner and Kapoor (2010) is the nearest, separating upstream components bundled by the focal firm from downstream complements bundled by customers, which suggests that the AI model is an upstream component of the incumbent's own agent (S1) but the assistant is a downstream channel for the incumbent's artifact (S2).

### 2.2 Openness and control: a calibrated choice, not a switch

Parker and Van Alstyne (2018) model the choice formally: closing the platform raises the sponsor's ability to charge for access, while opening raises developer ability to build on it; optimal openness is interior and depends on how long developers keep IP rights. Boudreau (2010), on 21 handheld computing systems, shows that granting access to independent developers accelerated device development up to fivefold, while devolving control added a positive but much smaller effect. The practical reading is that most of the benefit of openness comes from access, not from giving up control. All the incumbents studied here grant access to artifact operations (read, generate, edit) while keeping control of the editor, the file format, the rendering engine and the account. This is exactly the "granting access" regime, and the literature predicts that it captures most of the value of opening.

Boundary resources theory (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013; Eaton et al., 2015; Karhu, Gustafsson & Lyytinen, 2018) offers the strongest lens for the 2024-2026 events, because MCP servers, ChatGPT apps, Figma's write-to-canvas tools, Gamma's Generate API and Grammarly's Agents SDK are all boundary resources: interfaces through which an owner enables third-party contribution (resourcing) while keeping control (securing). Three predictions follow. First, resourcing and securing coexist, so the access terms (plan restriction, rate limits, credits, sign-in) are the securing side and should appear together with the opening, not later. The case files fit: Figma ties MCP write access to the paid seat and rate-limits by plan; Adobe requires sign-in to "unlock generative capabilities"; Gamma meters generations through MCP like API calls. Second, boundary resources are retuned in a distributed process (Eaton et al., 2015), so changing terms are normal, not a sign of confusion: Figma's credit updates of December 2025 and Gamma's October 2026 API-on-Free decision and agent-endpoint pricing are examples. Third, openness that is too wide exposes the platform to exploitation (Karhu et al., 2018): the Android forks paper concerns forking the platform, but the analogue in agentic settings is that an agent can extract the artifact or the design system and bypass the host interface. Hydari and Muzaffar (2026, carried from the register) frame this as the "going headless" decision: ceding the interface to agents suits some firms and destroys value capture for others. This is the closest published statement of the thesis puzzle, and it is a preprint that I could not re-verify.

Benlian, Hilkert and Hess (2015) add that openness is perceived by complementors on several dimensions; Grammarly's Agents SDK in closed or private beta since October 2025 and the 2024 closure of the Text Editor SDK are poor on perceived openness and predict a thin supply of third-party agents. The case file confirms few listed publishers.

Economists reach the same place from another side. Hagiu and Wright (2019) explain when a principal should "control" a decision and when to "enable" an agent: enable where the agent's investment is decisive and hard to monitor. Applied here, S1 (own agent) is control; S3 (hosting) is enabling and is advisable where third-party domain knowledge (research, citation, sales data) is decisive; S2 is a case where the incumbent does not choose at all, because the assistant has the user relationship.

### 2.3 Why open to external agents: evidence on API openness and the logic of free access

Benzell, Hersh and Van Alstyne (2024) estimate, with difference-in-differences and synthetic control, that public firms adopting public APIs grew faster, interpreting the result as an "inverted firm" that shares data and captures part of third-party surplus. This is the only quantitative evidence in the stream that linked openness to firm value, and it predates LLM agents. Its limitations for our case are clear: it covers publicly listed firms and a different technology, and gains come from third-party apps adding users, not from agents consuming functions. A companion working paper, "The paradox of openness: exposure vs. efficiency of APIs" (title only verified), signals the countervailing exposure effect. Evans and Basole (2016) is a short viewpoint, not an empirical base.

For access on free terms, Parker and Van Alstyne (2005) show that a firm can rationally give a product away in perpetuity if another side pays. In the cases, free entry through the assistant makes sense if the assistant is a demand channel for a paying side: Canva (mass freemium with search-intent heritage), Adobe Express ("free to ChatGPT users globally", Express MAU tripled) and Gamma (API keys on all plans). The model also says that free access is conditional on a monetisable follow-on, which explains why the generation step is metered even when the connector is free: it is the cost side that cannot be given away (Bergemann, Bonatti & Smolin, 2025, on LLM pricing; metadata only, unverified claim).

Hagiu and Wright (2024) on marketplace leakage describe the other side: a seller who is discovered on a platform wants to transact off the platform to avoid fees. The assistant-to-native-app flow ("Open in Adobe Express", Canva as "final mile") is a deliberate form of leakage that the incumbent wants. The assistant, if it had a fee model, would try to prevent it. At the time of this review no fee is evidenced in the cases, which means leakage is currently tolerated by the AI platforms; Hagiu and Wright (2025) on "platform traps" warn that dependence on a dominant intermediary becomes a trap when an outside option worsens as others join, and the dynamic pricing then follows. This is the sharpest warning in the literature for the free-entry cases.

### 2.4 Complementor strategy and platform-owner entry

The empirical literature on platform-owner entry is not unanimous. Zhu and Liu (2018) find that Amazon enters successful product spaces and avoids those requiring seller-specific investment; entry discourages seller growth. Wen and Zhu (2019) find that Android developers facing a Google entry threat cut innovation and raise prices, shifting innovation to unaffected apps. Foerderer et al. (2018), using Google Photos, find the opposite sign: entry was associated with a 9.6% increase in major updates by affected apps. Gawer and Henderson (2007) explain why owners cannot enter everything: Intel encouraged entry by committing credibly not to squeeze. Li and Agarwal (2017) show that first-party integration (Facebook-Instagram) changes demand for third-party apps; this is the mechanism of self-preferencing. Seamans and Zhu (2014) show newspapers reacting to a cross-side entrant by raising subscription prices and differentiating.

Three predictions for the thesis follow. (1) Envelopment risk is highest where artifacts are commodity and effort-light: simple slides, social graphics, document drafts. Zhu and Liu predict that a general assistant enters those spaces, which matches the AI-native Gamma and the low end of Canva and Adobe Express. (2) Relationship-specific investment protects: the more the artifact depends on an editing surface, a file format, a library or a collaboration graph, the less the assistant enters. Figma (design files and Dev Mode), Adobe (Creative Cloud) and Coda (documents and tables) have these. (3) Because the empirical result on innovation is ambiguous (Foerderer et al.), a defensible reading is that the complementors' response depends on the type of complement; the cases should not be read as proof of either "crowding out" or "spurring".

Platform envelopment (Eisenmann, Parker & Van Alstyne, 2011) is the theory that names the strategic threat. An envelopper in one platform market enters another by bundling its functionality with the target's, harnessing shared user relationships and "foreclosing an incumbent's access to users". An AI assistant holds the user relationship across tasks and can bundle image, slide, document and code generation. Creation tools are complements or weak substitutes in the typology. The defence follows from the theory: own the user relationship (the freemium base of Canva, 100 million Gamma users, Adobe's more than 900 million Acrobat and Express MAU), bundle (Superhuman suite, Adobe suites, Canva Affinity and code), or keep the artifact where the work continues. The cases show all three defences used at once alongside S2, which suggests that S2 is never used alone.

Multihoming (Cennamo, Ozalp & Kretschmer, 2018) predicts that complements built for several platforms are of lower quality because specialising to each architecture is costly. MCP reduces the per-platform specialisation cost (one server for many clients) but ChatGPT apps, Copilot agents and Gemini extensions each have distinct UI requirements. Adobe, which built separate packages for ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude and Gemini, is the test case; Figma's single MCP server is the opposite pole. I could not find a systematic comparison of per-host quality.

Appropriability (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012; Huang et al., 2013) predicts that firms with strong downstream assets or IPR gain less from joining a platform and demand better terms, while firms without them gain most. Applied to the cases: Gamma, with the fewest downstream assets, opens widest and earliest (all plans, including Free); Adobe and Canva, with large brands and distribution, open on their own conditions; Figma opens but ties it to the paid seat. The same logic would predict the highest terms for Grammarly, whose assets are embedded in other applications, which indeed fits its choice to host rather than to be hosted. Note the register already holds Ceccagnoli et al. as LENS06; the extension here is to derive a ranking of expected terms.

### 2.5 Hosting (S3): governance, generativity, first-party content

The literature is consistent that hosting requires governance of quality and risk. Wareham, Fox and Cano Giner (2014) show that ecosystems must balance stability and evolvability. Cennamo and Santaló (2019) show that high generativity increases variance in how complements satisfy users and can lower value creation. Rietveld, Schilling and Bellavitis (2019) show that platform sponsors use selective promotion to manage ecosystem value. Hagiu and Spulber (2013) show that first-party content is used to solve expectation problems when the platform is new: the platform must seed supply to attract the other side. Grammarly's Agent Store lists eleven agents "By Grammarly" beside partner agents, which is consistent with first-party seeding, and its rebrand explicitly puts Grammarly "among many agents". Mayer et al. (2025) on GenAI as a boundary resource in an educational platform predict that GenAI changes governance and shifts power between owner and complementors; they support treating governance as a lever (rival hypothesis R-governance) but concern a content platform, not an agent store.

The theory also gives a reason why S3 is so rare among creation incumbents. Hosting requires an installed base large enough that third parties benefit from reaching it, and a product where agents operate inside the user's workflow. Grammarly's overlay across about a million apps provides this. Figma and Canva host plugins and apps (a long tradition), not agents; the hosting of agents inside the design surface is not yet observed. Tiwana (2015) and Tiwana et al. (2010) suggest that the architecture of the host (modularity, input control) decides whether complements can evolve; no evidence on this for agents exists.

### 2.6 Generative AI platforms and their complementors

This literature is young. Heimburg, Schreieck and Wiesche (2025), an embedded case study of complementors in the OpenAI ecosystem, identify four mechanisms by which complementors cope with GenAI's open-endedness and inscrutability (system instructions, context data, curating inputs, revising outputs) and two logics (reap and differentiation). The relevance to S4 is direct: context data is how complementors differentiate. The limits are as important: complementors are small builders, not incumbents with their own multi-million user base, and the study predates agents calling tools through MCP (claims from the register; unverified in this stream). Mayer et al. (2025) is similar. Azoulay, Krieger and Nagaraj (2025) argue that formal IP and secrecy will not prevent entry in GenAI, while control of key complementary assets such as compute will concentrate markets; applied downstream, the assets that matter to a creation incumbent are not models but brand context, templates, file formats and distribution. Jacobides, Brusoni and Candelon (2021) show upstream concentration in AI among Big Tech, which makes the incumbents price-takers on tokens and gives AI platforms a double role as supplier and channel.

On MCP itself the base is technical. Hou et al. (2026) define MCP as an open standard for bidirectional communication between models and tools and map its threat model; a measurement study of MCP marketplaces (arXiv 2509.25292, snippet only) reports that 50.9% of crawled entries were invalid or low value, which signals a supply glut and weak discoverability. For a firm like Figma or Canva, this is the argument for entering the curated directories of ChatGPT and Claude, where review and verification create scarcity (the Claude directory documents verification for "security, reliability, and compatibility"; snippet only; no monetisation terms found). Rondina et al. (2026) and a CMR piece (snippet only) argue that agents are a new intermediation layer and gatekeeper; a position paper (Lin et al., 2025) argues that attention-based superplatforms must counter agents. The contrast is analytically informative: platforms whose asset is user attention resist agents, while creation tools whose asset is the artifact welcome them. This asymmetry is not yet theorised.

The trade-press claims that the Apps SDK makes ChatGPT a gatekeeper controlling discovery and monetisation could not be sourced to authors and are not used as evidence; they correspond, however, to the envelopment and self-preferencing literatures (Eisenmann et al., 2011; Li & Agarwal, 2017) and would be testable once ranking and revenue-share rules are public.

### 2.7 What is contested, and what is missing

Established: access-based openness delivers most of the gains (Boudreau, 2010); boundary resources embody the control-contribution tension (Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013); platform owners enter successful, low-specificity spaces (Zhu & Liu, 2018); assets and capabilities decide who benefits from joining (Ceccagnoli et al., 2012). Contested: whether owner entry crowds out or spurs complementary innovation (Wen & Zhu, 2019 versus Foerderer et al., 2018); whether APIs create growth outside the cases studied (Benzell et al., 2024 is one empirical study). Missing: any study of access terms (free, plan-gated, metered) in agent ecosystems; any analysis of incumbents with their own installed base who are complementors of an AI assistant while also hosting agents; evidence on the revenue flows between assistants and tool vendors; and the first tests of envelopment by AI assistants of creation tools.

**Figma (S2 strong, S3 weak).** The boundary-resource and access-versus-control theories explain the seat-anchored terms: access is granted to the artifact, while control stays in the paid seat and plan limits. Zhu and Liu predict that Figma is protected by artifact-specific investment, and the case reports that MCP-using large customers grew Full seats about 70% faster, which supports the "complement raises demand" logic of Li and Agarwal (2017) and Parker and Van Alstyne (2005). The theory does not explain why Figma has no agent host despite a 10,000-plugin community: Wareham et al. and Cennamo and Santaló give a candidate (governance and generativity costs), untested.

**Canva (S2 strong, S3 weak).** Free or low-friction entry fits the cross-side logic of Parker and Van Alstyne (2005) and the leakage logic of Hagiu and Wright (2024) ("final mile"). Appropriability theory predicts a better bargaining position for Canva than for small vendors, consistent with early-mover launch partnerships. The case file warns that third-party data question the size of the LLM channel, which would weaken the case for dependence but not the platform-trap risk.

**Adobe Express (S2 strong, S3 limited).** Adobe is the multihoming test: separate packages for ChatGPT, Copilot, Claude, Gemini. Cennamo et al. (2018) predict per-host quality variance; no data. The exclusion of Teams, Enterprise and Education plans from the ChatGPT app is a securing choice in the boundary-resource sense. The Q2 FY2026 statement that agent monetisation is "basically about credit consumption" matches the metered terms literature only loosely (no theory of credits was found).

**Grammarly/Superhuman (S3 strong, S2 only through suite).** The best fit is with hosting theory: first-party seeding (Hagiu & Spulber, 2013), governance (Wareham et al.; Mayer et al., 2025), and the observation that its overlay is the asset third-party agents lack. Platform envelopment explains why it is not a tool for external agents: Grammarly's value is the surface at the cursor; as a callable function it would be bypassed. The decision to open only Docs and Mail, where artifacts live, and to charge on paid plans matches Parker and Van Alstyne (2018) on charging for access. Weaker fit: the SDK reversal in 2023-2024 (closing a boundary resource) and a later reopening as Agents SDK show retuning (Eaton et al.), but the literature has nothing on a firm that closes and reopens.

**Gamma (AI-native benchmark; S2 strong).** The highest fit with the appropriability and platform-trap logic: with no downstream assets beyond its user base, it opens everywhere, on every plan, and charges for generation. Zhu and Liu predict the greatest exposure, since decks are commodity artifacts. Its pricing of agent endpoints "by the work the agent does" has no counterpart in the platform literature and belongs to the outcome-based rival hypothesis.

**The puzzle.** All four incumbents open within two years, with different terms. The literature provides necessary conditions (benefit from access, retained control, relationship-specific assets) but not a model that predicts which terms. The difference between "free entry with metered generation" (Canva, Adobe, Gamma) and "paid plans only" (Grammarly suite, Figma seat-anchored) lines up with the presence of an installed paid base that the incumbent wants to protect against cannibalisation, which is a hypothesis of this review, not a finding.

## 4. Implications for the thesis

**Propositions supported.**
- P-S2: incumbents with artifact-specific assets (formats, collaboration, libraries) open to external agents as complementors, granting access and retaining control through seat, plan and credit terms (Boudreau, 2010; Parker & Van Alstyne, 2018; Ghazawneh & Henfridsson, 2013).
- P-terms: the access terms are the securing side of a boundary resource and will be retuned within months (Eaton et al., 2015). The cases confirm the retuning, so the thesis should treat the terms as a time series, not a snapshot.
- P-defence: S2 is paired with bundling and own user relationships because of envelopment risk (Eisenmann et al., 2011).
- P-S3: hosting is chosen by the firm whose asset is a distribution surface, not an artifact (Hagiu & Wright, 2019; Hagiu & Spulber, 2013; Gawer & Cusumano, 2014).

**Propositions challenged.** (a) That opening to AI platforms is a defensive move only: Benzell et al. (2024) and Parker and Van Alstyne (2005) support an offensive growth logic. (b) That owner entry necessarily crowds out complementors: Foerderer et al. (2018) contradicts. (c) That platforms always resist agents: Lin et al. (2025) argue they must, yet creation incumbents do the opposite.

**Strategies informed.** S2 and S3 directly; S4 (Heimburg et al. 2025, Azoulay et al. 2025: context and complementary assets are where complementors keep value); S5 (cost-driven metering follows from the upstream concentration in Jacobides et al. 2021, and from LLM pricing models in Bergemann et al. 2025); S6 (bundling as an envelopment defence); R-governance (Mayer et al., 2025; Wareham et al., 2014).

**Gaps the thesis can claim.**
1. No study treats incumbents with their own installed base as complementors of LLM assistants, rather than small builders (Heimburg et al., 2025).
2. No theory of access terms for agent consumers (free, plan-gated, metered) beyond classic two-sided pricing; the cases give a first typology.
3. No evidence on the dual position of being both complement to an AI platform and host of third-party agents (Grammarly).
4. Asymmetry between attention platforms (resist agents) and artifact platforms (welcome agents) is observed but untheorised.
5. No empirical test of envelopment by AI assistants of creation tools; the thesis can offer a pre-registered set of indicators (user relationship, commodity versus specific artifact).

**Caveats.** Many entries are metadata only. The Apps SDK and Claude directory terms are not documented in verified sources; the thesis should collect them from primary pages before the data freeze (30 November 2026).
