## 2. Thematic synthesis

### 2.1 What a business model is, and how "fit" is treated

The literature agrees on the usefulness of the business model as a unit of analysis but not on its definition (Zott, Amit & Massa, 2011; Massa, Tucci & Afuah, 2017; Wirtz et al., 2016). Three families are visible. The first treats the business model as the architecture of value creation, delivery and capture (Teece, 2010; Chesbrough & Rosenbloom, 2002), the second as an activity system (Zott & Amit; Amit & Zott, 2010), and the third as a set of choices and consequences (Casadesus-Masanell & Ricart, 2010). The course framework (WHO, WHAT, HOW, ECONOMIC MODEL; Parolini, 2021) is closest to the first family, and through Coda's entrepreneurial formula (Coda, 2012) and Normann's business idea (Normann, 1977) it adds an explicit requirement of consistency among the components. Whether Parolini cites Coda or Normann in the text remains unverified (academic_notes section 1.1). Osterwalder and Pigneur (2010) list nine blocks and leave fit implicit.

Fit is treated in three ways. Siggelkow (2002) models fit as a developmental process in which elements are thickened, patched, coasted or trimmed. This is useful for the thesis, because "depth" of change (SQ2) can be coded as thickening (more of the same, such as a new AI feature inside the seat model) versus patching (a new core element, such as metered credits or a third-party agent channel). Demil and Lecocq (2010) argue that consistency must be dynamic: a firm survives by keeping a changing set of components mutually reinforcing, and temporary inconsistency is a normal part of evolution. Casadesus-Masanell and Ricart (2010) add that choices (pricing policy, asset ownership, governance) create consequences that feed back into the model, so governance terms are part of the model, not an add-on. What is missing is an empirical account of how consistency is restored when one component, the marginal cost of production, changes sharply, as happens when inference replaces human effort. The software-economics literature (Lehmann & Buxmann, 2009; Cusumano, Kahl & Suarez, 2015) treats SaaS as a near-zero marginal cost business; none of the sources I found models inference cost explicitly.

### 2.2 Business model innovation and adaptation in incumbents

Foss and Saebi (2017) review 150 articles and conclude that the field lacks construct clarity and an account of antecedents, contingencies and outcomes. Their companion piece (Foss & Saebi, 2018) distinguishes wicked from paradigmatic problems, which matters because incumbents with stable routines face a more paradigmatic problem than entrants. Saebi, Lien and Foss (2017) find that perceived opportunities and threats drive adaptation, moderated by strategic orientation (survey; content from memory, BM012). Chesbrough (2010) names the barrier: a new model conflicts with the logic of the existing one, so firms need experimentation and a strategic bridge. Sosna et al. (2010) show that models emerge by trial and error rather than by plan.

The incumbent-specific work is the most useful for the thesis. Kim and Min (2015) show that incumbents adding a new business model perform better when the new model is added early where assets are complementary and in an autonomous unit where assets conflict. Markides and Charitou (2004) give the contingency logic (separate, integrate, or abandon). Velu and Stiles (2013) stress cannibalisation rules. Khanagha, Volberda and Oshri (2014), the closest analogue to SaaS-to-agent transition because it follows a large ICT firm moving to cloud, find that simple spatial separation fails and that firms oscillate between separated and integrated modes. Cozzolino, Verona and Rothaermel (2018) trace a publisher after the internet and show that adaptation ran through opening a closed model into an open platform-based one. Snihur, Zott and Amit (2021) add the appropriation dilemma: gaining ecosystem legitimacy invites imitation. These studies are all single-case or small-sample, dominated by one industry each, and none examines a discontinuity in which the technology performs the customer's own work.

### 2.3 Incumbent response to technological discontinuities

The classical results are well established and contested at the same time. Tushman and Anderson (1986) distinguish competence-enhancing from competence-destroying discontinuities; Henderson and Clark (1990) show incumbents fail at architectural innovation because their information filters follow the old architecture; Christensen (1997) and Christensen and Bower (1996) add resource-allocation and customer power. Tripsas (1997) shows that incumbents with specialised complementary assets can survive; Tripsas and Gavetti (2000) show that cognition and identity can block the use of such assets (content from memory for both). Hill and Rothaermel (2003), Gilbert (2005), Danneels (2004) and Eggers and Park (2018) refine or critique these claims. The disruption debate (Christensen, McDonald, Altman & Palmer, 2018; Danneels, 2004; Kumaraswamy, Garud & Ansari, 2018) concludes that the theory is better at saying who is vulnerable than at predicting outcomes, and that responses are underexplored. Ansari, Garud and Kumaraswamy (2016) reverse the perspective: the disruptor needs the support of incumbents, which creates coopetition. Eggers and Park (2018) organise incumbent response into awareness, motivation and ability, a frame that fits cross-case differences better than any single theory.

Dynamic capabilities (Teece, 2007; Teece, 2018) and ambidexterity (O'Reilly & Tushman, 2013; Raisch & Birkinshaw, 2008) provide the capability and structural side. Teece (2018) makes business model design itself a dynamic capability. The evidence that ambidexterity works in digital transitions is mixed (Khanagha et al., 2014). Practitioner claims that incumbents "simply need to become AI-native" are rarely linked to this evidence.

### 2.4 Platform and ecosystem logic: the incumbent as host, tool and competitor

S2 and S3 move incumbents toward platform logic. Gawer and Cusumano (2014) and Rietveld and Schilling (2021) establish the dynamics; Cusumano (2010) argued that SaaS becomes a platform only if vendors open their infrastructure. The platform-owner-entry literature gives mixed predictions for hosting third-party agents while building one's own: Zhu and Liu (2018) find owners enter the best complementor spaces; Wen and Zhu (2019) find complementors reduce innovation and raise prices; Foerderer et al. (2018) find that entry raised complementary innovation through attention spillovers. Adner and Kapoor (2010) and Giessmann and Legner (2016) show that ecosystem value depends on the progress of complementors and on aligning the platform model with the legacy model. None of these studies treats complementors that are AI agents with their own owner; this is the closest unexplored territory for the S2/S3 distinction.

### 2.5 Digital and AI business model innovation, 2019 to 2026

The AI business model literature is large but mostly conceptual, case-based on providers, and pre-agentic. Åström, Reim and Parida (2022) trace a three-phase process for an AI provider; Sjödin et al. (2021) and Burström et al. (2021) study industrial firms; Kanbach et al. (2024) derive six propositions on generative AI from a scoping review of 513 data points that includes software engineering; Jorzik et al. (2024) review the field; Teng, Ye and Martinez (2025) study value propositions in IT firms. Lee et al. (2019) and Haefner et al. (2021) are earlier and broader. Verganti, Vendraminelli and Iansiti (2020) argue that AI moves design decisions from humans to machines, which is directly relevant to creation tools. Iansiti and Lakhani (2020, book, [M]) predict that firms must rebuild their operating architecture around data and learning.

The only source that explicitly theorises agentic AI as an economic actor in business model change is Najmaei and Sadeghinejad (2026, preprint), which distinguishes supporting, generative and agentic logics and predicts a shift from episodic redesign to continuous adaptation. It offers no test. Tagliapietra Sanabria (2026, SSRN) argues that SaaS regresses to infrastructure and value migrates to whoever guarantees outcomes. Both are propositions, not evidence, and the second is a single-author working paper. Peer-reviewed evidence on outcome-based business models comes from manufacturing (Sjödin et al., 2020) and shows that success needs agreed value distribution and a measurable outcome, which creation-oriented SaaS rarely has. This is a checkable reason why none of the five cases has adopted outcome-based pricing.

Practitioner and analyst claims diverge from this academic picture. A search snippet from trade press (not opened) attributes to IDC the prediction that pure seat-based pricing will be obsolete by 2028 and to Gartner a large amount of enterprise spend exposed to agentic substitution; ICONIQ's 2026 survey is cited for AI gross margins near 52%. These are [V-snippet] only and are handled in the PR and L1 streams. The academic literature offers no equivalent quantified prediction, and its pricing theory (Lehmann & Buxmann, 2009; Wagner, Benlian & Hess, 2014; Holm & Günzel-Jensen, 2017) predates variable AI cost.

### 2.6 SaaS business model literature

Peer-reviewed work on the SaaS business model is thinner than the practitioner discussion. Cusumano (2008, 2010) and Cusumano, Kahl and Suarez (2015) describe software firms' move from products to services; Choudhary (2007) models licensing versus SaaS incentives; Giessmann and Legner (2016) and Khanagha et al. (2014) study vendors' cloud transitions; Ojala and Tyrväinen (2011) and Stuckenberg (2014) are minor. On freemium, Wagner, Benlian and Hess (2014) link conversion to the perceived fit of the premium offer, Holm and Günzel-Jensen (2017) give strategic conditions for success, and Kumar (2014, HBR, [V-snippet]) frames the balance between free and paid features. All assume negligible marginal cost of the free tier. Product-led growth, seat expansion and net dollar retention are not treated in the peer-reviewed sources I found.

### 2.7 Contested points

1. **Competence-enhancing or destroying.** Agentic AI can strengthen incumbents' complements (design systems, brand kits, templates, installed base) or destroy the skill barrier on which tools were built. Tushman and Anderson (1986) and Tripsas (1997) give both readings; empirical resolution is not available.
2. **Separate or integrate.** Kim and Min (2015) and Markides and Charitou (2004) recommend separation under conflict; Khanagha et al. (2014) and Khanagha, Volberda, Sidhu and Oshri (2014) show it often fails for core technologies.
3. **Disruption or sustaining.** Danneels (2004) and Christensen et al. (2018) disagree on predictability.
4. **Open or closed.** Cozzolino et al. (2018) support opening; Zhu and Liu (2018) and Wen and Zhu (2019) warn about owner entry.
5. **Seat versus outcome.** Theoretical works favour outcome logics (Najmaei & Sadeghinejad, 2026; Tagliapietra Sanabria, 2026), manufacturing evidence is cautionary (Sjödin et al., 2020).

**Reading of the table.** The BM-theory predictions on cost-driven capture, rationing, new core elements and acquisition mode fit the cases well, and the fit is strongest for Figma and Canva. Evidence supports the gradual patching story (Siggelkow) more than the incumbent-blindness story (Henderson & Clark; Tripsas & Gavetti): all four incumbents moved within about two years. The classical inertia theories explain better why Adobe Express and Grammarly lag in agent GA than why they move on S2. The literature fails to explain three things: (i) the external-agent channel (S2) as a distribution decision, because platform theory assumes complementors, not buyers' agents; (ii) the Grammarly pattern of being absorbed into a suite, which sits between acquisition and renaming and is not covered by the dual-model literature; and (iii) Canva powering a competitor (Claude Design), a coopetition case beyond Ansari et al. (2016).

## 4. Implications for the thesis

**Propositions supported.**
- *P-fit.* Incumbents change the ECONOMIC MODEL first (credits, caps, add-ons), and other components follow with delay (Demil & Lecocq, 2010; Siggelkow, 2002). Cases support the first half; the second half (WHO/HOW) is the longer-term test.
- *P-seat anchor.* Seats persist because of existing-model barriers (Chesbrough, 2010; Christensen & Bower, 1996). Supported in all four incumbents.
- *P-open.* Opening toward external agents is an incumbent defence (Cozzolino et al., 2018). Supported, with the exception of Grammarly's S2.
- *P-acquisition mode.* Acquisition mode depends on asset conflict (Kim & Min, 2015). Descriptively supported.

**Propositions challenged or qualified.** (a) The predictive reading of disruption theory (Danneels, 2004). Gamma behaves like a new-market entrant, but incumbents have not lost the seat anchor, so a "SaaSpocalypse" reading is not supported by the theory alone. (b) Outcome-based models (Najmaei & Sadeghinejad, 2026; Tagliapietra Sanabria, 2026): no adoption, and the peer-reviewed evidence says preconditions are demanding. (c) Simple separation of AI-native units (Khanagha et al., 2014): the cases show both parallel and integrated modes within one firm.

**Strategies informed.** S1 (Henderson & Clark, 1990; Verganti et al., 2020; Tripsas & Gavetti, 2000); S2 and S3 (Cozzolino et al., 2018; Ansari et al., 2016; Zhu & Liu, 2018; Wen & Zhu, 2019; Gawer & Cusumano, 2014); S4 (Tripsas, 1997; Teece, 2018; Gregory et al., 2021, partly); S5 (Teece, 2010; Wagner et al., 2014; Holm & Günzel-Jensen, 2017; Demil & Lecocq, 2010); S6 (Cusumano et al., 2015; Siggelkow, 2002); S7 (Kim & Min, 2015; Markides & Charitou, 2004; Khanagha et al., 2014); R-outcome (Sjödin et al., 2020); R-governance (Casadesus-Masanell & Ricart, 2010; thin).

**Gaps the thesis can claim (L4).**
1. No empirical study of how creation-oriented SaaS incumbents change business models when agents produce the artifacts; AI-BMI work is on providers and industrial firms, and the agentic papers are conceptual.
2. No treatment of "external agent as customer-side actor" in business-model terms; platform-owner-entry research covers complementors, not buyers' agents.
3. Fit among components when marginal cost is no longer near zero is not modelled; the thesis can offer an operational coding of depth (feature versus patch) using the course framework.
4. Product-led growth and seat expansion are absent from the peer-reviewed literature found.
5. Access terms for agents (rate limits, plan-tied access) as a business model component are untheorised.
These claims hold only within my search and verification limits (Crossref plus two web checks). Before the thesis asserts "no study exists", repeat the search in Scopus or Web of Science.
