## 2. Thematic synthesis

### 2.0 What the literature is, and is not

Three points frame everything below. First, no peer-reviewed study was found that examines S1, S4, S6 or S7 in creation-oriented SaaS under agentic AI; the academic register (7 November) reached the same conclusion for the wider topic. The strategies are therefore studied here by borrowing mechanisms from servitization, complementary assets, bundling and envelopment, technology acquisitions and ambidexterity, all developed in manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, chemicals, early internet and platform markets. Second, the most relevant newer items are recent and mostly preprints (Hydari and Muzaffar, NEW02 and NEW03; Klotz, NEW04), already in the register. Third, practitioners (Bain, G01; Sequoia, G13; a16z, ST028) make strong claims about moats and services that the academic theories qualify. The synthesis compares both.

### 2.1 S1: own agent inside the product, and AI-native product transformation

**Definition and theory.** S1 means that the incumbent places an agent that acts on the artifact inside its own product (Figma agent, Canva AI 2.0, Adobe Express AI Assistant, Superhuman Go). Four literatures speak to it.

1. *Servitization.* Vandermerwe and Rada (1988, ST001) describe firms adding services to goods until the service dominates the offer. Baines et al. (2009, ST002) and Kowalkowski et al. (2017, ST003) review the field. The agent moves a creation tool from "you operate the tool" to "the product does the work", which is a service logic delivered by software. The practitioner version is "services as software" (Foundation Capital, ST016; Sequoia, G13). Fang et al. (2008, ST082) give the only large-sample finding: firm value stays flat or slightly negative until services reach 20% to 30% of sales and then rises, and the move pays more when the service is related to the core and the firm has slack resources. Oliva and Kallenberg (2003, ST004) stress how hard the organisational transition is.
2. *Human-AI division of labour.* Raisch and Krakowski (2021, ST005) argue that automation and augmentation are interdependent and that choosing one side creates tension over time. Vaccaro et al. (2024, ST013), on abstract evidence and memory, find that human-AI combinations do not beat the best of human or AI alone on average, with creation tasks as a favourable exception [M for the moderator]. Fügener et al. (2022, ST014) find that combinations work when the AI delegates to humans, not when humans delegate to the AI. Dell'Acqua et al. (2023, ST011, snippet) show a "jagged frontier". These imply design choices: drafts for review, proactive nudges, editable artifacts.
3. *Design as decision making.* Verganti, Vendraminelli and Iansiti (2020, ST008) treat design as the human decision side of innovation that AI can now take over. This is the best theoretical fit for design tools that become decision infrastructure.
4. *Make, borrow or buy.* Capron and Mitchell (2009, ST006) model the choice among internal development, alliances and acquisition as depending on capability gaps and internal frictions. Korinek and Vipra (2024, ST007) argue that the foundation-model layer concentrates. Cursor's Composer (ST017, snippet) shows an application firm post-training its own model, with disputed claims on how far.

**Predicted conditions for adoption.** From these sources: (a) the artifact is structured enough for an agent to act on parts of it (components, variables, layers), so the agent can do multi-step edits a general assistant cannot; (b) a general assistant threatens to substitute the product's core task (Noy and Zhang, 2023, ST010: ChatGPT already does mid-level writing); (c) the firm has the resources and a related service to bear a flat or negative phase (Fang et al.); (d) supplier dependence and inference cost push toward first-party or post-trained models, as the make-borrow-buy logic and the concentration argument imply; (e) the human-AI design keeps the person in the loop where delegation is badly calibrated.

**Evidence in the cases.**
- Figma: Figma AI (26 Jun 2024, F04) on third-party models; Make GA (24 Jul 2025, F12); agent in Figma Design launched to everyone 23-24 Jun 2026 (F29), GA and credit-consuming on 6 Oct 2026 (F32); "more than doubled the share of work powered by our own post-trained design model" between 1 Jul and 31 Oct 2026 (F35). The IPO prospectus of 31 Jul 2025 admitted reliance on "off-the-shelf foundational models" (F13). Condition (a) is explicit: Figma files are structured, and the agent works with the team's design system (F25).
- Canva: Magic Write (7 Dec 2022, CV-01), Canva AI and Canva Code (10 Apr 2025, CV-11), Canva Design Model (30 Oct 2025, CV-17), AI 2.0 with scheduling that creates only drafts for review (16 Apr 2026, CV-27), Craft image model (22 Oct 2026, CV-36), Brush design-editing model, "46% cheaper per edit" (29 Oct 2026, CV-37). The reliance on frontier models was named a cost problem in the Q2 2026 shareholder update (CV-31).
- Adobe Express: AI Assistant announced 28 Oct 2025 (AE-12), still beta on 22 Oct 2026 (AE-31), after going outside first (GPT Store, 1 Apr 2024, AE-07).
- Grammarly: GrammarlyGO (9 Mar 2023, GR-01), Go 1.0 with triggers (12 Aug 2026, GR-29), proactive writing agents (9 Oct 2026, GR-31).
- Gamma: AI-native from 27 Mar 2023 (GA-01); Gamma 5 agent for all plans (6 Oct 2026, GA-16); agent work priced by the work done (5 Oct 2026, GA-15).

**Counter-evidence.** Make Designs was withdrawn on 2 Jul 2024 after reproducing Apple's Weather app (F05), an example of the output-similarity risk discussed in the copyright literature (Samuelson, 2023, ST076; Henderson et al., 2023, ST077). Grammarly's Expert Review was withdrawn in March 2026 (GR-23). Canva's agentic features reached users late and partly, and growth guidance was cut from 30% to 20% in 2026 (CV-31). Adobe's agent has been in beta for over twelve months. Doshi and Hauser (2024, ST012) find that generative AI raises individual creativity while reducing collective diversity, which explains why Canva taught its platform to adapt library designs rather than generate new ones (CV-32) and challenges the claim that more AI output differentiates. Brynjolfsson's Turing trap (2022, ST015) is a conceptual warning against replacing rather than complementing people. No filing in the cases separates AI revenue, so Fang's 20% to 30% threshold cannot be tested.

**Open questions.** Does an embedded agent shift the offer from tool to service in revenue terms, or only in features? Is the first-party model a cost play (Canva: "30x cheaper") or a differentiation play (Figma: "design model")? Does proprietary-model investment pay when general models improve fast? Almost nothing in the literature covers app-layer firms that post-train models.

### 2.2 S4: serving proprietary context and complementary assets

**Definition and theory.** S4 means that the firm turns assets it holds (brand kits, design systems, templates, documents, usage history) into context that agents read or write, inside and outside the product. The theory is Teece's profiting from innovation (1986, ST018): who captures value depends on appropriability, the dominant design and complementary assets (generic, specialised, co-specialised). Teece (2018, ST019) and Helfat and Raubitschek (2018, ST021) extend it to platform ecosystems, where complement providers may capture substantial value. Tripsas (1997, ST020) is the closest design-industry analogue: typesetter incumbents with specialised assets (type libraries) and capabilities survived discontinuities. Rothaermel (2001, ST022) shows incumbents exploiting complementary assets through alliances with entrants. Teece, Pisano and Shuen (1997, ST023) add the capability to reconfigure assets.

A second strand asks whether data confers advantage. Gregory et al. (2021, ST024) define data network effects, user value that grows with the number of users whose data trains an AI service. Hagiu and Wright (2023, ST026) show that data-enabled learning gives an advantage only under conditions (learning that does not saturate, advantage hard to imitate). Their HBR piece (2020, ST027, summary page read) says people "grossly overestimate the advantage that data confers". The a16z essay (Casado and Lauten, 2019, ST028, read) argues that most "data network effects" are scale effects with falling marginal value and higher acquisition cost, and lists verticalisation, go-to-market, account control and brand as better sources of defensibility. Bain (G01) says "your data is your moat". Switching costs (Klemperer, 1995, ST030) are the third strand. Technical context work (Lewis et al. 2020, ST031; Liu et al. 2024, ST033; Mei et al. 2025, ST032; Anthropic, 2025, ST034) shows what context means in practice: retrieval, selection and curation, not bulk.

**Predicted conditions.** S4 is predicted when (a) the firm holds assets that agents need but cannot generate, which are specialised or co-specialised and not easily replicated (brand kits, design systems with component logic, proprietary template libraries); (b) the core feature is imitable, so appropriability is weak; (c) serving the asset does not erase switching costs faster than it creates distribution; (d) the asset improves with use, which would create data effects, but only if learning does not saturate (Hagiu and Wright). Structured context should beat raw dumps (Liu et al.).

**Evidence in the cases.**
- Figma: Dev Mode MCP server (4 Jun 2025, F10), remote MCP (Q3 2025, F14), write to canvas with the team's design system (24 Mar 2026, F25), Specs and Guidelines as "a native home for context" (14 Oct 2026, F34). Large customers using MCP grew Full seats about 70% faster (Q1 2026 press release, as recorded in the case file).
- Canva: Canva MCP server and deep research connector (26 Jun 2025, CV-14), Brand Kits applied in Claude (Jan 2026, CV-21) and in ChatGPT (5-6 Feb 2026, CV-22); context rationed (Free Brand Kit cut to three colours, CV-23; Business capacity from 1,000 to 100 kits, CV-19).
- Adobe Express: weak at the Express level; templates surface in assistants and hand off to Express (AE-07, AE-15), brand context stronger at Adobe level.
- Grammarly: style guides and brand tones (GR-08), Coda Brain (GR-11), Fathom meeting context (GR-30), Docs MCP (8 Jul 2026, GR-28); Grammarly itself has no official MCP.
- Gamma: custom MCP connectors for enterprise admins (GA-15); context is the user's, not Gamma's.

**Counter-evidence.** Under Casado and Lauten's argument, volume of data is not a moat, and the cases give no disclosed evidence of data network effects: Canva's usage numbers are self-reported (CV-22) and an independent measure contradicts the channel's size (CV-14 to CV-22 notes; Similarweb). Serving assets outward may reduce lock-in: an agent that reads a design system can rebuild it elsewhere, and Anthropic's Claude Design builds a design system "by reading your codebase and design files" (F27). Brand features at Grammarly predate generative AI. Klemperer's switching-cost logic cuts both ways: assets that lock users in can be exported to the assistant that then owns the relationship. Hydari and Muzaffar (NEW02) make this the central risk of "going headless".

**Open questions.** Which of the assets is specialised (hard to replicate) and which generic? Does context rationing (Canva Brand Kit limits) monetise or merely ration? Is there any evidence that usage data from agent interactions improves a first-party model (a data network effect), or is S4 purely a static-asset strategy?

### 2.3 S6: expanding scope (suites, code, marketing)

**Definition and theory.** S6 broadens what the product produces or the roles it serves: from artifact to outcome (a campaign, an app, a work suite). Theory comes from four places. *Bundling*: Bakos and Brynjolfsson (1999, ST035; 2000, ST036) show that bundling many information goods is profitable when marginal costs are very low because aggregation makes demand predictable; Nalebuff (2004, ST037) shows that a multiproduct incumbent can use the bundle as an entry barrier against a better single-product entrant. *Envelopment*: Eisenmann, Parker and Van Alstyne (2011, ST038) show how a provider in one platform market enters another by combining functionality in a multi-platform bundle that leverages shared users, foreclosing the incumbent's users. The same logic describes the threat that general assistants pose. *Scope economies*: Teece (1980, ST039) holds that scope pays when shared, non-tradable assets are reused; Tanriverdi and Venkatraman (2005, ST040) link performance to knowledge relatedness. *Platform-owner entry*: Gawer and Henderson (2007, ST041), Zhu and Liu (2018, ST042) and Wen and Zhu (2019, ST043) study when a platform owner enters complementors' markets and how complementors react. A fifth strand on end-user programming (Ko et al., 2011, ST045) and code-assistant productivity (Cui et al., 2026, ST046; Sarkar and Drosos, 2025, ST047) explains why code is an adjacent space. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019, ST048) frame tasks reallocated to agents, a conceptual base for "from artifact to outcome".

**Predicted conditions.** S6 is predicted when (a) marginal cost is low enough for bundling to work, a condition that AI inference weakens; (b) the firm shares users and assets with adjacent markets (envelopment needs shared users); (c) the adjacent market is where value is moving ("when code is a commodity", F28; marketing when "the cost of making creative heads to zero", CV-29); (d) a threat of envelopment by assistants makes broadening defensive. Against it: feature fatigue (Thompson et al., 2005, ST044) and organisational complexity.

**Evidence in the cases.**
- Figma: Make (7 May 2025, F09; GA 24 Jul 2025), Payload CMS acquisition (17 Apr 2025, F08, "native backend" Oct 2026), code layers in open beta (14 Oct 2026, F34), Sites and Buzz (still beta at 7 Nov 2026), Bud acqui-hire (7 Jul 2026, F30), Weave media generation (F15).
- Canva: Affinity (Mar 2024, CV-05) and free relaunch (30 Oct 2025, CV-20); Canva Code (CV-11); Canva Grow (30 Oct 2025, CV-18; Grow 2.0 Jun 2026, CV-29); MagicBrief, MangoAI, Ortto (CV-13, CV-24, CV-26). Rationale from Grow's head: what is left when creative cost falls is customer acquisition.
- Adobe Express: bundled into Acrobat Studio (19 Aug 2025, AE-10) and Acrobat Express (6 May 2026, AE-21); Express becomes a creation layer of the Adobe suite. This is the Bakos and Brynjolfsson pattern at the portfolio level; Microsoft's January 2025 bundling of Copilot into consumer Microsoft 365 (ST049, snippet) is the platform-incumbent version.
- Grammarly: Coda (Dec 2024 agreement, GR-11), Superhuman Mail (1 Jul 2025, GR-14), rebrand to a suite (29 Oct 2025, GR-17), Rows, Fathom; Enterprise sold "through Superhuman Go" (GR-26). Atlassian's USD 610 million purchase of The Browser Company (ST050, snippet) is a comparator.
- Gamma: Gamma Imagine (17 Mar 2026, GA-11) moves into graphics against Canva and Adobe.

**Counter-evidence.** Nalebuff's result needs a stable bundle, but Adobe unbundled Express from Photography and Acrobat plans in 2024-25 and re-bundled it in Acrobat Studio (AE-08, AE-10): the bundle is not stable. Bakos and Brynjolfsson assume near-zero marginal cost, while Figma's non-GAAP gross margin fell from 92% (FY2024) to 82% (Q3 2026) and Canva called its AI cost per task too high. Feature fatigue and complexity appear in Canva's restructuring of 4 Nov 2026 (1,271 roles; "our growth has slowed for the first time in a decade", CV-40). Figma's Sites and Buzz remain beta 18 months on. Grammarly's scope moves partly leave the creation category (email, meeting notes). Zhu and Liu's finding that platform-owner entry can chill complementors raises a risk for Figma and Canva if they host agents and then build in their niches.

**Open questions.** Is scope expansion a defence against envelopment or a distraction? Can envelopment theory be reversed: when an assistant builds the same artifact, the incumbent's counter-move is to bundle workflow and distribution (Grow) rather than the artifact? Is the right level of analysis the suite (Adobe, Superhuman) rather than the product?

### 2.4 S7: acquiring AI-native companies, integrated or in parallel

**Definition and theory.** S7 means buying AI-native firms or teams, and either integrating them or running them separately. Ahuja and Katila (2001, ST051) establish that technological acquisitions raise innovation output when knowledge relatedness is moderate [M for the shape]. Kaul (2012, ST057) shows that technological innovation is followed by portfolio reconfiguration: complementary-resource-seeking acquisitions and divestment of noncore businesses. The integration literature gives the central trade-off: integration enables coordination but can destroy the capabilities that made the target valuable (Puranam, Singh and Zollo, 2006, ST052; Puranam, Singh and Chaudhuri, 2009, ST053; Puranam and Srikanth, 2007, ST054). Paruchuri et al. (2006, ST063) document productivity losses after integration. Graebner (2004, ST055) shows the role of acquired leaders. The seller's side is in Graebner and Eisenhardt (2004, ST056). Acqui-hires: Coyle and Polsky (2013, ST059), Chatterji and Patro (2014, ST058, content [M]) and Ng and Stuart (2022, ST060). Pre-emption: Cunningham, Ederer and Ma (2021, ST061). Digital-sector base rates: Gautier and Lamesch (2021, ST062). The mode of expansion is studied by Villalonga and McGahan (2005, ST064). Structural separation draws on March (1991, ST068), Tushman and O'Reilly (1996, ST069), Raisch and Birkinshaw (2008, ST070), Markides and Charitou (2004, ST071) and the register items BMI05 and BMI06.

**Predicted conditions.** Acquisition is predicted when (a) the capability gap is wide and speed matters (Capron and Mitchell); (b) the firm has resources (cash from an IPO, a termination fee, revenue-based capital); (c) targets are small and early, so autonomy preserves capability (Puranam et al.); (d) the target has a distinct business model (dual business models call for separation: Markides and Charitou); (e) the aim is talent, in which case the product is shut (Coyle and Polsky). Integration is predicted when the target's knowledge must be exploited quickly in the acquirer's product (Puranam and Srikanth).

**Evidence in the cases.**
- Figma: Diagram (21 Jun 2023, F01), Modyfi asset purchase (USD 14.0 M, 7 Apr 2025, product closed 13 Jun 2025, F07), Weavy (USD 85.3 M plus USD 43.8 M equity, closed 3 Oct 2025, kept partly in parallel, F15), Bud/Orchids acqui-hire (7 Jul 2026, both products closed by 18 Jul 2026, F30). The pre-AI Adobe deal (15 Sep 2022, about USD 20 B, abandoned 18 Dec 2023) is documented by the CMA's provisional findings (ST065).
- Canva: Leonardo.ai (29 Jul 2024, "will continue to run independently", technology integrated, CV-08); MagicBrief (18 Jun 2025, integrated into Grow, CV-13); MangoAI and Cavalry (23 Feb 2026, CV-24); Simtheory (integrated) and Ortto (standalone) (8 Apr 2026, CV-26).
- Grammarly: Coda (agreement 17 Dec 2024; closed Jan 2025), Superhuman (1 Jul 2025), Rows (23 Feb 2026; product closed 31 May 2026), GPTZero (23 Jun 2026; site kept), Fathom (14 Sep 2026), financed by a USD 1 B revenue-linked facility (29 May 2025, GR-13).
- Adobe Express: none at product level (Semrush, Topaz at Adobe level).
- Gamma: Lica (date unverified, GA-13).

**Counter-evidence.** Theory fits the integration split well, but cleanly: parallel operation where the target has its own users and model (Leonardo.Ai, Ortto, GPTZero, Weave) and integration or shutdown where the target is a team or a feature (Modyfi, Rows, Bud, MagicBrief). That is a hypothesis consistent with Puranam and Srikanth, not a test. Killer-acquisition logic does not fit: the closures in the cases look like talent capture, not pre-emption of a rival (Cunningham et al. use pharmaceutical pipelines with portfolio overlap). Ahuja and Katila and Paruchuri et al. predict capability loss after integration, but no outcome data exist for the cases; deal values are mostly undisclosed. Several "AI-native" targets are not AI-native in a strict sense (Coda, Superhuman Mail predate generative AI; Serif is not AI). The regulatory dimension is thin in the cases but real: the CMA treated hiring a team with know-how as a possible merger in Microsoft/Inflection (ST066, snippet) and found AI, prosumer tools and mobile a weak constraint on Adobe in its Figma review (ST065, para 42), a judgment overtaken by events. The FTC staff report (ST067, snippet) shows how deep ties between model suppliers and clouds are, which bears on the partner option.

**Open questions.** Are small AI-native acquisitions a substitute for building models (Canva's Craft draws on Leonardo researchers) or a hedge? What happens to retention of acquired teams (Ng and Stuart) in the cases? Does the integrated-or-parallel choice track the target's maturity, as Puranam et al. imply, or the acquirer's cash and urgency? Does the 2022 Adobe-Figma deal count as a response to technological change, or to competition in design (the CMA reading)?

### 2.5 Rival hypothesis: governance as a strategic lever

**Definition and theory.** The hypothesis is that trust, safety and responsible-AI positioning (licensed training data and indemnification, authorship and provenance, admin controls) differentiate the product and can be monetised. Mäntymäki et al. (2022, ST074) and Papagiannidis et al. (2025, ST075) give the vocabulary for organisational AI governance; Jobin et al. (2019, ST073) show a shared vocabulary of principles, which implies that principles alone do not differentiate. Samuelson (2023, ST076) and Henderson et al. (2023, ST077) establish the copyright risk behind "commercially safe" claims. Hydari and Muzaffar (NEW03) propose "accountability assets", complementary assets that make AI output legitimate and auditable, which would connect S4 and governance. Mayer et al. (NEW01) treat governance as a platform mechanism.

**Evidence in the cases.** Adobe: Firefly launched as "commercially safe", trained on licensed content, with IP indemnity for enterprise customers from June 2023 (ST080, snippet; AE-03, AE-04). Canva: Shield, with AI indemnification for enterprise and USD 200 M for creators over three years (4 Oct 2023, CV-03), and Enterprise addressing "AI tool creep" (23 May 2024, CV-07). Figma: Governance+ with AI hosting controls, prompt discovery logs and MCP call logs (F37, date of AI controls unknown) and credit governance (F38). Grammarly: Authorship (Oct 2024, GR-09; agent-specific attribution 10 Mar 2026, GR-24) and the GPTZero purchase as an "authenticity layer" (23 Jun 2026, GR-27). Gamma: none found.

**Counter-evidence and boundary conditions.** Detection is technically fragile and biased: GPT detectors misclassify non-native English writing (Liang et al., 2023, ST078, content [M]) and detection is easy to evade (Sadasivan et al., 2023, ST079). That challenges an "authenticity layer" built on detection and favours process-based records such as Authorship. Regulation may erase the differentiator: Article 50 of the AI Act applies from 2 August 2026, with a machine-readable marking relief reported until 2 December 2026 for systems already on the market (ST081, snippet; sources conflict), turning provenance into a baseline. Grammarly's Expert Review (March 2026, GR-23) shows that governance failures cost more than the lever earns. In Figma, governance is a paid enterprise add-on and an admin tool, framed as compliance and cost control, not as the core strategy. No filing reports revenue from any governance feature, so the hypothesis cannot be tested on monetisation.

**Open questions.** Is governance differentiating (willingness to pay, win rates) or a hygiene factor? Does it matter more where the buyer is accountable (enterprise, education) than in mass freemium? Does an external agent bypass the governance the incumbent sells (BYOK, audit logs of MCP calls)?

### 2.6 How the strategies interlock

The literature suggests dependencies rather than independent choices. Complementary assets (S4) are the reason a partner or acquirer wants the incumbent (Rothaermel) and what makes a broader bundle valuable (Nalebuff); scope expansion (S6) uses shared users and assets (Eisenmann; Teece 1980) and is usually done through acquisition (S7; Kaul 2012); the agent (S1) needs context (S4) to differ from a general assistant, and first-party models depend on make-borrow-buy (Capron and Mitchell). Governance cuts across: it protects the asset base and the enterprise segment. What the literature does not explain is the sequencing observed in the cases: Adobe Express and Canva moved outward before inward (GPT Store April 2024 versus an agent still in beta), and Figma moved from outward access (MCP 2025) to a first-party agent (2026). Existing theory treats own agent and external access as separate lines of business; the cases show them as a single portfolio decision.

## 3. Anchoring to the cases

**Figma.** Best explained by Teece-style asset logic (design systems as specialised complementary assets; F25, F34), Eisenmann's envelopment as the threat (Claude Design, 17 Apr 2026; new risk factor 14 May 2026) and by the make-borrow-buy logic for its first-party model. S7 follows the "talent and integrate" pattern (Puranam et al.) with one parallel exception (Weave). The Adobe deal of 2022 is a pre-AI baseline for acquiring a threat (CMA). Not explained: why write access remained a free beta for seven months with only an announced usage price; why Sites and Buzz stay in beta.

**Canva.** The clearest case of a firm that both builds and buys models (Capron and Mitchell): Leonardo (buy, parallel), Craft and Brush (build), OpenAI (partner, 2 Nov 2026). Brand Kits are rationed context. Scope (Grow, Code) fits envelopment and platform-owner entry, but the 4 Nov 2026 restructure and the growth cut (CV-31, CV-40) illustrate feature fatigue and the limits of bundling when marginal cost is not zero. Governance (Shield) is an early differentiator that later became a baseline.

**Adobe Express.** Explained by bundling and portfolio logic rather than an Express strategy: Express as a component of Acrobat Studio and an export of Adobe's templates into assistants. Complementary assets are exported, not guarded (AE-07, AE-15). Governance ("commercially safe", indemnity) is Adobe-level. The weak S7 and weak S1 (beta) are consistent with a challenger within a larger group. The freemium-over-ARR trade (Q2 FY26, AE-23) is not captured by the strategies at all.

**Grammarly (Superhuman).** The best match for S7 and S6 as a combined suite play (five deals in 21 months, GR-11 to GR-30), and for acquired leaders as integrators (Graebner); the platform-owner side (Agent Store) reflects S3, covered by another stream. Governance is its most distinctive lever (Authorship, GPTZero), exposed to the detection-validity critique. Many acquired firms predate generative AI, so "AI-native acquisition" needs a narrower definition.

**Gamma.** The benchmark for AI-native S1: agent work is the metered unit, models are third-party, scope moves into graphics (GA-11). Casado and Lauten's warning applies: a start-up's data is not its moat, and Gamma's defence is growth and product speed. No S4 or governance evidence, because the sources are thin (founder claims).

## 4. Implications for the thesis

**Propositions supported or qualified.**
- P-S1: Incumbents adopt an embedded agent when the artifact is structured and a general assistant threatens substitution; the literature predicts a flat or negative value phase before servitization pays (Fang et al.). Supported by the cases in direction and unobservable in value.
- P-S4: Value sits in assets the agent needs but cannot produce (Teece; Tripsas). Qualified by the data-moat critique: assets must be specialised, not merely voluminous. The cases show assets being served outward and rationed at once, which theory does not predict.
- P-S6: Broadening protects against point-solution entrants (Nalebuff) and is a form of envelopment (Eisenmann), but bundling assumes low marginal cost, which inference breaks. The cases show restructuring (Canva) and unstable bundles (Adobe).
- P-S7: Small acquisitions follow integrate-or-separate logic (Puranam et al.; Markides and Charitou). Killer-acquisition and large-deal theories fit the pre-AI Adobe-Figma episode, not the 2025-26 pattern.
- P-Gov: governance differentiates where accountability is bought (enterprise, education) and is eroded by regulation and by the weakness of detection. Evidence of monetisation is absent.

**Strategies informed.** S1, S4, S6 and S7 and the governance hypothesis directly; S2, S3, S5 only through interactions noted in 2.6.

**Gaps the thesis can claim.**
1. No peer-reviewed study of creation-oriented SaaS combining S1, S4, S6 and S7 under agentic AI; theory is transferred from other sectors.
2. Existing acquisition theory studies targets as technology or talent; none addresses AI-native targets whose value is a model or a distribution surface.
3. Complementary-asset theory (Teece) does not address assets that are simultaneously served to rivals' agents and rationed to users.
4. Servitization theory predicts a value threshold (20% to 30% of sales); AI service revenue is not separately disclosed, so the transition is observable only in business-model terms (WHO/WHAT/HOW/ECONOMIC MODEL).
5. The sequencing of outward access before inward agents (Adobe Express, Canva) has no theoretical account.
6. Governance as a lever is argued conceptually (NEW03) but not tested.
