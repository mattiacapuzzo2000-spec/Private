## 2. Thematic synthesis

### 2.1 What "agentic" means, and where the generative phase ends

**Established.** There is broad agreement in the technical literature that the move from generative AI to agents is a move from producing content to taking actions with tools. Sapkota et al. (2026) place generative AI as the precursor, AI agents as modular, LLM-driven and task-specific systems that use tools, and agentic AI as systems with multi-agent collaboration, task decomposition and persistent memory. Kolt (2025) describes the same transition in legal terms, from "generative models that can produce synthetic content" to agents that "plan and execute complex tasks with only limited human involvement". Biilmann (2025), writing as a practitioner, puts it in one sentence: the generative aspect gets the attention, but "the largest disruption ... will come from bringing agency to computers". The two standard surveys of LLM-based agents (Wang et al., 2024; Xi et al., 2025) describe agents as entities that sense, decide and act, which is the textbook notion inherited from Wooldridge and Jennings (1995) and Russell and Norvig (the latter marked [M] here).

Anthropic's (2024) "Building effective agents" supplies the distinction most used by builders: workflows run LLMs through predefined code paths, whereas agents let the model direct its own process and tool use. This matters for the thesis because many product features described as agents by the five cases would count as workflows under this definition. Parasuraman et al. (2000) and the newer levels of autonomy proposed by Feng et al. (2025) give the graded view: autonomy is a design decision, expressed as the role the user takes (operator, collaborator, consultant, approver, observer), and it is separable from model capability. Berente et al. (2021) and Baird and Maruping (2021) supply the information systems versions: autonomy is a managerial variable, and agentic artifacts are "no longer passive tools waiting to be used", which turns use into delegation.

**Contested.** Abou Ali et al. (2026) warn against "conceptual retrofitting", the equating of today's neural, prompt-driven agents with the symbolic agents of the 1990s. Gartner's "agent washing" claim (2025; only about 130 of thousands of vendors are said to be genuinely agentic) is the practitioner version of the same worry, but it is a prediction based on a webinar poll, not a measurement (AG046). There is no agreed threshold for when a feature stops being generative assistance and becomes agentic. The practical solution in the cases is to code "agency" levels 0 to 3; Feng et al. (2025) could give that coding an external scale, and I flag that none of the autonomy papers has been tested on commercial productivity software.

**Missing.** I found no IS or strategy paper that operationalises the generative/agentic boundary at product level for software vendors. Kumar, Wei and Zhang (2026) may do so, but I could not read it.

### 2.2 Generative versus agentic effects on business models

This is where the thesis's two-phase framing (generative 2023 to 2024, agentic 2025 to 2026) meets the least direct literature. Four threads can be assembled.

*Conceptual separation.* Najmaei and Sadeghinejad (2026) distinguish three logics of AI-enabled business model innovation: AI as instrument, AI as co-creator (generative) and AI as delegated economic actor (agentic). Bohnsack and de Wet (2025) argue that agentic AI "can increasingly execute the core mechanisms of value creation, delivery, and capture", so that AI becomes the strategy itself. Rothschild et al. (2025) offer the clearest economic version: early generative gains are "largely confined to predefined tasks within existing workflows", while the more profound impact lies in reducing communication frictions between consumers and businesses once assistant agents and service agents interact programmatically. Gerlach (2026) and Prole and Prole (2026) add organisational and boundary versions (agentic AI moves AI from information to action; execution becomes modular faster than accountability). These are all conceptual, mostly 2025 to 2026 preprints or mid-tier journals, and the cases they use are illustrative or hypothetical. They agree on direction, but none provides tests.

*Productivity evidence from the generative phase.* The strongest empirical evidence concerns assistants: a 15% productivity gain in customer support concentrated among novices (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025), a 40% time reduction and 18% quality gain on professional writing (Noy and Zhang, 2023), the "jagged frontier" in consulting tasks (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023), and individual creativity gains with collective sameness in stories (Doshi and Hauser, 2024). The common implication for software vendors is that generative AI lowers the skill barrier, which is the supervisor's "anyone can do it" point. The Noy and Zhang result bears directly on Grammarly's core, since a general assistant does the writing improvement that was Grammarly's differentiator.

*Evidence on the shift to delegation.* The best usage data are vendor-held. Anthropic's Economic Index (2025) reports that "directive" conversations rose from 27% to 39% and that automation exceeded augmentation for the first time, with 77% of business API use classified as automation. Anthropic's (2026) autonomy study shows the longest Claude Code sessions roughly doubling in three months and users auto-approving more with experience. These support the claim that behaviour moves from augmentation toward delegation, but they come from one model provider that is also a competitor of the five cases, and they concern coding more than creative work.

*Coding as the leading indicator.* METR's randomised trial found that early-2025 tools made experienced developers 19% slower (Becker et al., 2025), while its February 2026 update reports data too contaminated by selection to quantify a speedup yet "likely" positive (METR, 2026). The reversal inside one year is itself informative: the effect of the same product category changed within the thesis's agentic window, so any productivity claim must be dated to a tool generation.

**Contested.** Whether the agentic phase changes the business model or only adds features is open. Bohnsack and de Wet and Najmaei and Sadeghinejad say business models change; Klotz (2026) and Hydari and Muzaffar (2026a, b) say the effect is conditional on application type and on accountability assets. The thesis's SQ2 (depth of change) addresses precisely this.

**Missing.** No paper compares the same firms before and after the agentic turn in business-model terms. Generative-phase studies measure task productivity, not vendor revenue models.

### 2.3 Agents as users and customers of software

**Interfaces for agents.** Yang et al. (2024) argued that language-model agents are "a new category of end users" needing purpose-built interfaces, and showed an agent-computer interface improving success on software engineering tasks. Lù et al. (2025) generalise this to the web: build an Agentic Web Interface rather than forcing agents through human-designed pages. Biilmann (2025) gave it a name, agent experience (AX), by analogy with Norman's UX. The AX literature is entirely practitioner writing; my search found no peer-reviewed definition or measure of AX. The practical content is consistent across practitioner sources: discoverable APIs, clear documentation, reliable calls, recoverable errors. The Postman (2025) survey suggests demand outpaces readiness (about one in four developers design APIs with agents in mind), but it is a vendor survey that I could only read through summaries.

**Machine customers and agentic commerce.** Gartner's machine customer thesis (Scheibenreif and Raskino, 2023, in the grey register as G12) was extended in October 2025 to a forecast that by 2028 90% of B2B buying will be agent intermediated (Gartner, 2025b; wording varies in secondary coverage). Stripe and OpenAI's Agentic Commerce Protocol (2025) shows one concrete design: the agent initiates a checkout through a scoped payment token while the merchant remains seller of record. Academic work is conceptual or simulated. Shahidi et al. (2025) argue that agents lower search and contracting costs but create congestion and price obfuscation, and that outcomes depend on "whether agents operate within or across platforms". Bansal et al. (2025) find in a simulated market that agents show first-proposal bias giving 10 to 30 times advantages to speed over quality, a result that suggests being fast and callable may matter more than being best. Cherep et al. (in the academic register as NEW14) similarly find agents are biased choosers. Guimaraes Figueira (2026) names the risk to suppliers "brand erasure": the agent may omit or substitute the brand at the moment of action.

**Contested.** Whether external agents will really be the buyers of SaaS is unproven. All figures are forecasts; no study of an agent-initiated software purchase was found. Consumer-commerce evidence (McKinsey commentary found in search, not included as a source) points to agents helping evaluate options while the final decision stays human.

**Access terms are a battleground.** Pattison et al. (2026) document that platforms "block and degrade agent access, often in secret" and argue for a normative infrastructure distinguishing malicious bots from delegated agents. Examples are Salesforce's 2025 changes to Slack API terms, reported to bar bulk export, persistent indexes and some LLM use (Hunton, 2025; Glean said it could only retrieve data in real time), and Amazon's suit against Perplexity's Comet shopping agent, where a March 2026 injunction was reversed on appeal (press coverage, AG073). These are the cases of the same logic, and both are grey, secondary-source evidence. They show that incumbents choose between S2 (open to external agents) and gatekeeping, and the choice can differ by data type.

### 2.4 Headless software, API-first design and disintermediation of the interface

The conceptual core is Hydari and Muzaffar (2026a): general-purpose agents unbundle the application, and "going headless" (ceding the interface, exposing expertise as callable services) "is correct for some firms and destructive for others". Their second paper (2026b) names the mechanism that can protect integrated firms: accountability assets, complementary assets that make AI outputs legitimate, auditable and assignable. Prole and Prole (2026) arrive at a compatible argument, that execution modularises faster than accountability. Klotz (2026) applies transaction cost and resource-based reasoning to the "SaaSocalypse" and concludes that the make-or-buy shift depends on application type. All four are 2026 preprints, which limits confidence, and all concern enterprise or vertical software.

The incumbent's own statement of the headless strategy is Salesforce Headless 360 (2026): capabilities exposed "as an API, MCP tool, or CLI command", with the claim that a platform requiring humans to click through UIs "is not ready for the Agentic Enterprise". That announcement is a company claim without outcome data, but it shows a large SaaS vendor treating the interface as optional, while keeping the platform, permissions and control tooling (which is where the Hydari and Muzaffar argument predicts value capture is retained). Nielsen's (2023) account of intent-based outcome specification, the shift from GUIs to natural-language interfaces, remains the usual citation for the interface paradigm, though he expected a hybrid in which GUI elements remain; this is an opinion piece and I read only a summary.

**Missing.** None of these papers addresses creation-oriented tools whose product is an editable artifact. The thesis's cases suggest the artifact itself (a layered, editable file) is a reason why the interface may not be ceded: the agent generates, the human edits in the product. No source tests this.

### 2.5 Protocols and their strategic meaning

**Facts.** Anthropic open-sourced MCP on 25 November 2024 as a universal standard for connecting assistants to data sources (Anthropic, 2024). Google launched A2A on 9 April 2025 with over 50 partners and explicitly as a complement to MCP (Google, 2025). OpenAI's Apps SDK (6 October 2025) is built on MCP and counted Canva and Figma among pilot partners (OpenAI, 2025; snippet only). In parallel, OpenAI and Stripe released the Agentic Commerce Protocol (2025). On 9 December 2025 MCP was donated to the Agentic AI Foundation under the Linux Foundation with AWS, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI and others as platinum members (Linux Foundation, 2025). Anthropic (2025b) claims more than 10,000 active public servers and 97 million monthly SDK downloads.

**Empirical academic work.** There are three measurement or security studies. Hasan et al. (2025) analyse 1,899 open-source servers and report that 7.2% have general vulnerabilities and 5.5% show MCP-specific tool poisoning. Guo et al. (2025) crawl six marketplaces and find that "more than half of listed projects are invalid or low-value", with dependency monocultures; this is a useful counterweight to the vendor-reported count. Hou et al. (2025) map the server lifecycle and a threat taxonomy. Ehtesham et al. (2025) compare MCP, ACP, A2A and ANP descriptively.

**Strategic meaning.** The sources support three readings. (1) MCP is moving towards neutral infrastructure, which lowers the lock-in risk of S2 for vendors. (2) Whoever runs the agent client (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot) controls discovery, and the Apps SDK makes the client a distribution platform; Shahidi et al. (2025) make the "within or across platforms" question central. (3) Security and governance exposure grows with openness, which supports governance as a differentiator. I found no academic study of vendor strategy around MCP servers, which AG034 and AG035 do not address; the strategic reading in practice comes from trade commentary, which I excluded as promotional, and from company disclosures in the case files.

### 2.6 Evidence on adoption and limits

**Adoption surveys are high on interest, low on scale.** McKinsey (2025) reports that 62% of organisations are at least experimenting with agents (23% scaling, 39% experimenting), with scaling usually limited to one or two functions (snippet-level; sample not verified). Deloitte (2025) reports 30% exploring, 38% piloting, 14% with solutions ready and 11% in production (secondary coverage). The MIT NANDA (2025) claim that about 95% of generative AI pilots show no measurable P&L return is widely quoted, but it concerns generative pilots, uses a narrow success definition and has contested methods. Stanford HAI (2026) reports organisational AI adoption at 88%, a figure that measures any use, not agents. Gartner's cancellation forecast (40% of agentic projects by 2027) belongs in the same family of claims: forecasts with a clear direction (hype exceeds scale) but weak method. I rate this body of evidence as consistent with "interest far ahead of production", not as a measurement.

**Capability benchmarks diverge by task type.** On realistic business tasks agents are still weak: CRMArena-Pro reports around 58% single-turn and 35% multi-turn success (Huang et al., 2025, from Salesforce's own lab); the Remote Labor Index finds a best automation rate of 2.5% on real freelance projects (Mazeika et al., 2025); tau-bench found under 50% task success and poor consistency (Yao et al., 2024). Against this, GDPval reports frontier models "approaching industry experts in deliverable quality" on tasks built by professionals (Patwardhan et al., 2025, from OpenAI). The divergence is largely explained by task definition (single deliverable versus end-to-end project) and by who wrote the benchmark. Kapoor et al. (2024) add that agent benchmarks neglect cost.

**Productivity in creative and design work.** The only controlled evidence on a case product is Stewart et al. (2026): access to Figma Make cut completion time by about 20% among those completing the tasks, with larger gains for product managers than for designers. That pattern (gains skewed to non-specialists) fits the generative-phase pattern in support and writing work (Brynjolfsson et al., 2025; Noy and Zhang, 2023) and the "anyone can do it" framing. Yun et al. (2026) report that an organising agent shifted designers' effort from arranging to curating content. Both are small preprints.

**Missing.** No study measures how often creation-SaaS users let an external agent produce an artifact instead of using the product, which is the demand-side fact the thesis needs.

### 2.7 Value creation and capture for software vendors

The conceptual claims converge on a short list. (a) Value shifts from the interface to context, accountability and orchestration (Hydari and Muzaffar, 2026a, b; Prole and Prole, 2026; Choudary, 2025, not read). (b) Seat logic is weakened if agents do work that seats did (Brynjolfsson's 2022 "Turing trap" distinguishes substitution from augmentation and is the usual frame for pricing a labour substitute; the pricing sources already in the registers cover this). (c) Cost of inference is a first-order variable (Kapoor et al., 2024). (d) Agents may reduce the supplier's ability to hold the customer relationship (Guimaraes Figueira, 2026; Shahidi et al., 2025; Pattison et al., 2026). The only direct empirical evidence on value capture is from case material and company disclosures, not from this stream's academic sources.

**Peer-reviewed versus practitioner claims.** Peer-reviewed papers I found mostly concern the generative phase and worker-level productivity (Science, QJE, Science Advances). Everything about agents and business models is preprint, working paper or practitioner-sourced. Practitioners (Gartner, McKinsey, Deloitte, Salesforce, Anthropic) agree with the direction of academic conceptual work but supply forecasts and self-reported usage, not tests. The most useful hybrid sources are those that publish data and method: METR, Anthropic's usage studies, Postman, Stanford HAI.

## 3. Anchoring to the cases

- **Figma.** Evidence in the case file shows S2 (MCP server, Claude and ChatGPT apps), S4 (design systems as agent context) and S5 (credits). The literature explains S2 and S4 through the within-or-across platform logic (Shahidi et al., 2025) and Hydari and Muzaffar's (2026a) value capture argument; AX and SWE-agent (Yang et al., 2024) explain why a structured, machine-readable file format is an asset. Stewart et al. (2026) is direct evidence on Figma Make. The literature does not explain why Figma ties MCP access to the paid seat: no source covers access-term design, only Pattison et al.'s observation that terms of access are contested.
- **Canva.** The "final mile of editing" role and launch-partner status in the Apps SDK (OpenAI, 2025) fit the "walled garden versus open web of agents" tension in Rothschild et al. (2025) and the brand-erasure risk (Guimaraes Figueira, 2026): Canva serves Brand Kits to agents to remain the place where the brand appears. Doshi and Hauser (2024) on convergent output supports the value of brand context. The coopetition with Anthropic (Canva powering Claude Design) is not predicted by any source.
- **Adobe Express.** The case shows the broadest presence in external assistants. The headless logic (Salesforce Headless 360, 2026) parallels Adobe's "atomization into a creation layer"; Hydari and Muzaffar (2026a) would ask whether Adobe retains value capture or cedes it. No source addresses a freemium funnel being the real driver, which the case file raises as a rival explanation.
- **Grammarly.** Noy and Zhang (2023) explain the commoditisation of the core; the accountability-asset argument (Hydari and Muzaffar, 2026b) and Prole and Prole (2026) fit the authorship and AI-detection governance emphasis; A2A (Google, 2025) and the multi-agent failure literature (Cemri et al., 2025) are relevant to S3 and the Agent Store, but no source studies an overlay or cursor-position distribution asset.
- **Gamma.** The best match is the autonomous business model concept (Bohnsack and de Wet, 2025) and the generative-to-agentic logic of Najmaei and Sadeghinejad (2026). Gamma's API opened to all plans and agent endpoints "priced by the work the agent does" (case file) are an instance that these papers anticipate but do not document; no source discusses an AI-native benchmark of this kind.

## 4. Implications for the thesis

**Propositions supported.**
1. Generative and agentic phases differ in kind, not only degree (Rothschild et al., 2025; Najmaei and Sadeghinejad, 2026; Biilmann, 2025; Anthropic Economic Index, 2025). Support: moderate; conceptual and vendor data.
2. External agents are a new class of user whose needs shape product design (Yang et al., 2024; Lù et al., 2025; Baird and Maruping, 2021). Support: moderate for coding, weak for creation tools.
3. Access terms and interface choices are strategic (Pattison et al., 2026; Hydari and Muzaffar, 2026a; Salesforce, 2026; Hunton, 2025). Strategies informed: S2, S3, S4.
4. Governance and accountability can be a defence (Hydari and Muzaffar, 2026b; Prole and Prole, 2026; Kolt, 2025). Informs the rival hypothesis R-governance; fits Grammarly more than Figma or Canva.
5. Inference cost is a real constraint on agent economics (Kapoor et al., 2024). Informs S5.

**Propositions challenged or qualified.**
- Fast, broad enterprise adoption of agents: not supported by surveys (McKinsey, Deloitte, Stanford, Gartner) or benchmarks on end-to-end work (Remote Labor Index, CRMArena-Pro). The thesis should avoid describing 2025 to 2026 as a period of mass agent adoption; "agentic phase" refers to what vendors build and expose, not to what customers have scaled.
- "MCP is the standard so S2 is costless": measurement work shows many listed servers are low-value (Guo et al., 2025) and security risks are real (Hasan et al., 2025).
- Strong SaaSpocalypse: qualified by Klotz (2026) and the boundary papers, which condition outcomes on application type.

**Strategies informed.** S1: autonomy scales (Feng et al., 2025; Anthropic, 2026) and productivity evidence is mixed (METR). S2: protocols, access terms, security. S3: A2A, multi-agent failure modes, platform logic. S4: accountability assets, brand erasure, Doshi and Hauser. S5: cost constraint, Turing trap framing. S6 and S7: little direct literature in this stream.

**Gaps the thesis can claim.**
1. No empirical study of how creation-oriented productivity SaaS vendors change access terms, pricing and product scope when agents become users (all relevant work is conceptual, vertical-AI or enterprise).
2. No academic work on vendor strategy around MCP servers and apps in assistants; evidence is grey.
3. No operational definition of generative versus agentic at product level that can be applied to vendor moves (the case file's agency coding is a candidate).
4. No test of whether the artifact (an editable file) acts as a complementary asset that keeps the interface alive when agents can generate the content.
5. Agent-experience (AX) has no peer-reviewed definition or measure.
