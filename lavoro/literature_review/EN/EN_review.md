## 2. Thematic synthesis

### 2.1 What the barrier theory says, and what generative AI touches

The classical approach starts with Bain (1956): incumbents earn rents when new firms face scale economies, product differentiation, absolute cost advantages and capital requirements. Porter (1980, 2008) folds entry into five forces, where the threat of entry depends on these barriers and on expected retaliation. Karakaya and Stahl (1989) operationalize the barriers for entry decisions, and Caves and Porter (1977) show that barriers protect groups of firms and also lock incumbents inside them (mobility barriers). The course adds a sixth force, complementors, via Brandenburger and Nalebuff (1996), whose value net says the same player can be a complementor and a competitor, and Brandenburger and Stuart (1996), who tie a player's share of value to its added value. Lieberman and Montgomery (1988, 1998) add first-mover advantages (preemption, switching costs, learning) and disadvantages (imitation, market change). Switching costs and network effects (Klemperer, 1995; Farrell and Klemperer, 2007) explain why a product with an installed base can survive a superior entrant.

Read against this list, generative AI acts mainly on two barrier families. First, it lowers the capital and skill needed to build: the cost of writing code, interface layouts, slides or copy. Second, it lowers some switching costs, because an agent can convert files, recreate layouts and learn a new interface for the user. It acts weakly on the other families: differentiation (brand, templates, trust), distribution (installed base, default placement), network effects (collaboration, shared libraries) and customer-specific assets (design systems, brand kits). The digital-economics baseline in Goldfarb and Tucker (2019) is that replication and distribution costs fall toward zero; generative AI adds a twist that this literature does not cover, a positive marginal cost per use (inference), which is the S5 problem and a gap against that baseline.

This produces a barrier matrix that organizes the rest of the review (Table 1).

| Barrier | Effect of generative AI | Direction for incumbent | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capital and engineering effort to build a competing product | Strongly lowered | Weakens | Moderate (2.2) |
| Skill to produce the artifact (design, layout, writing) | Strongly lowered for users | Weakens the "non-designer" promise of Canva and Grammarly | Moderate (2.2, 2.3) |
| Switching costs from formats and learned interfaces | Lowered by agent conversion | Weakens, partly | Weak, mostly conceptual |
| Distribution and installed base | Not lowered; model labs and suites hold their own | Holds | Mixed (2.4) |
| Network effects and collaboration | Not lowered | Holds | Weak evidence either way |
| Customer-specific assets (design systems, brand kits, templates) | Contested: agents need them but can also rebuild them | Holds if served on own terms | Conceptual (2.5) |
| Data advantage | Weaker than assumed | Weakens | Moderate (2.5) |
| Variable cost per use | New cost for incumbents; also for entrants | Raises the cost of defending | Case evidence |

### 2.2 Evidence that generative AI lowers entry costs

The newest empirical work agrees on the direction. Kim, Kang and Song (2026) find a sharp rise in new ventures on Product Hunt after generative AI, driven mostly by solo founders, which is the supervisor's "anyone can do it" in data. Cao and Bhatia (2025) find that AI coding tools expand access to digital entrepreneurship for founders without managerial expertise and improve the performance of technical founders. Two firm-registry studies point the same way: Marchesi and Tang (2025) report a rise of more than 10% in firm formation in AI-compatible industries after ChatGPT, with experimentation cost as the mechanism; Cai and Gu (2025) report about 51,000 additional firms in China (about 6% of entry), concentrated in small firms with fewer executives. All of these are working papers or preprints, I read only summaries, and the platforms and populations differ (Product Hunt launches, registered firms). Taken together they are consistent evidence for "more and smaller entrants", not yet for "more successful entrants".

Two qualifications are already in the same papers. Kim et al. report that teams still dominate the top of the Product Hunt rankings (the team share in the Top 10 rose, on a figure that I could only see in an aggregator summary), so the bottleneck seems to move from starting to sustaining. Cao and Bhatia find an expertise complement: technical founders gain more. Entry is cheaper, but scaling still needs organization, which matches the Teece-style view that complementary assets decide who captures value.

At the individual level, field and lab experiments show productivity gains from generative AI, especially for less experienced workers (Noy and Zhang, 2023; Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2023; Cui et al., 2026), with a quality limit outside the "jagged frontier" (Dell'Acqua et al., 2026). These are mostly firm-internal results and say little about start-up formation; I use them as the micro-mechanism.

Practitioner and investor data describe the same phenomenon from the revenue side. Stripe's data on its 100 fastest-growing AI customers show a median of about 11 months to USD 1M of annualized revenue, about four months faster than a cohort of 100 top SaaS companies from 2018 (Stripe, 2025). Bessemer's State of AI (2025) separates "Supernovas" (about USD 40M ARR in year one, gross margin about 25%) from "Shooting Stars" (about USD 3M in year one, margin about 60%) and stresses fragile retention. Carta (2025, 2026) shows that capital concentrates in AI, with more than 60 cents of each venture dollar on its platform going to AI companies in Q1 2026. Company cases fit: Base44 was sold to Wix for about USD 80M upfront after about six months and with a very small team (Globes, 2025); Gamma reports USD 100M ARR, profit and about 50 staff (TechCrunch, 2025, in the Gamma case file); Lovable and Bolt are in the grey register (G32, G33). The evidence has three weaknesses: it is self-reported or investor-sourced, it covers survivors, and the comparison cohorts differ in era. RevenueCat coverage (low-quality secondary source) suggests AI consumer apps churn faster than other apps, and the Bessemer margin figure shows that fast revenue does not mean strong economics. So the entrants have visible growth, thin margins and unproven retention.

### 2.3 Counter-evidence: build cost has not collapsed

Three lines of evidence caution against reading "lower barriers" as "software is free". The first is productivity. The METR randomized trial with 16 experienced open-source developers found that allowing early-2025 AI tools increased task time by about 19%, while the developers believed they were faster (METR, 2025); METR itself says that it does not generalize, and a 2026 follow-up reportedly gives a weak and disputed reading (Bowley, 2026, a secondary source). Bain's survey reports 10% to 15% productivity gains for software teams and notes that coding is only 25% to 35% of the time from idea to launch (Bain, 2025). The second is quality: Veracode (2025) reports security flaws in 45% of AI-generated code tasks, and GitClear (2025) reports rising duplication and churn; both are vendor reports with a commercial interest. The third is demand-side: the Christensen and Adner-Zemsky logic that what matters is customer value, not supply cost (Adner and Zemsky, 2006; Christensen, Raynor and McDonald, 2015).

The peer-reviewed evidence on build cost is thinner than the grey evidence in both directions. The honest summary is: the cost of producing a first working version of a product has fallen sharply for small, greenfield, well-specified artifacts; the cost of running, securing, maintaining and supporting a product at scale has fallen much less. This is why the "SaaSpocalypse" argument (register G05, G07) and the "just-in-time software" idea need the distinction between building and operating.

### 2.4 Disruption, incumbents and who wins

Christensen's theory says that incumbents fail because resource dependence on current customers steers investment toward sustaining improvements (Christensen and Bower, 1996) and that disruptors start at the low end or in new markets (Christensen, Raynor and McDonald, 2015). Gans (2016) adds a demand-side reading: incumbents lose when the entrant's offer attracts their customers and they fear cannibalization; they can respond by self-cannibalization or by building a platform. Ansari, Garud and Kumaraswamy (2016) show that entrants who depend on incumbents' complements face a "disruptor's dilemma" and can be absorbed or co-opted; Henderson and Clark (1990) add that architectural change defeats incumbents whose routines encode the old architecture.

Applied to AI, the conceptual literature gives two opposite predictions that depend on the type of AI use. Agrawal, Gans and Goldfarb (2022) argue that "point solutions" (AI inside an existing workflow) favor incumbents, while "system solutions" that redesign decisions favor entrants because incumbents resist system change. Practitioner essays repeat this (Garg, 2023; Currier, 2023; Gil, 2023): incumbents win where AI is an add-on, startups where it changes the workflow; Tunguz (2023) and Lightspeed (2023) stress the distribution advantage, for example Microsoft Office's installed base. Stuart (2024) asks whether generative AI can end incumbent advantage, and Barney and Reeves (2024) argue that AI technology by itself will not give a sustainable advantage. These are essays with limited evidence, and several carry investor interests.

The most careful academic statement is Azoulay, Krieger and Nagaraj (2025): IP and secrecy will not protect model developers, while complementary assets (compute, data, distribution) keep the "old moats". Gans (2024) adds that market power in AI depends on whether data can be traded; Hagiu and Wright (2023) argue that data-enabled learning is a weaker source of advantage than network effects, which echoes a16z's earlier argument that data moats are over-promised (Casado and Lauten, 2019).

**What the theory predicts for the five cases.** Under a Gans-style reading, the incumbents' decisive question is whether agentic AI is a point solution (in-product agent, S1) or a system solution (an external agent that does the whole job and uses the incumbent as a tool, S2). Gamma, as an AI-native entrant that initially had no installed base, is the benchmark that the sustaining-versus-disruptive distinction is meant to test: it attacks the low end of presentation creation with a new workflow, which fits a new-market disruption, but it also relies on third-party models and so depends on suppliers that can enter its market.

### 2.5 Model labs entering applications: platform entry, envelopment and coopetition

The model labs are not classical entrants. They are upstream suppliers and, at once, owners of distribution (chat interfaces with hundreds of millions of users). The platform literature gives the closest theory. Platform owners enter complementors' markets when those are important and attractive (Gawer and Henderson, 2007; Zhu and Liu, 2018), complementors respond by retreating from threatened categories (Wen and Zhu, 2019) while innovation may continue elsewhere (Foerderer et al., 2018), and a platform with an overlapping user base can bundle a rival's functions and absorb its market (Eisenmann, Parker and Van Alstyne, 2011). Zhu and Iansiti (2012) show that installed-base advantage protects an incumbent only when the entrant's quality advantage is modest; large quality gaps overcome it. Economic models of vertical integration in AI point in the same direction: Korinek and Vipra (2024) argue that scaling pushes toward concentration and integration downstream, and a preprint calibration finds that integrated model providers reduce their own app price and raise their unintegrated rivals' price (Indika and Faheem, 2026; year to verify). Foundation Capital warns that model providers moved up the stack with first-party apps and changed API terms (Gupta and Garg, 2025), the "wrapper" problem in investor language. Thompson (2026) argues that agents use underlying software directly so that application interfaces, once a lock-in, may become unnecessary; this is a strong claim from a single analyst, known to me only through digests.

The relations are not only hostile. Claude Design (17 April 2026) uses Canva's Design Engine and exports to Canva, PDF, PPTX and HTML, and no Figma export is listed (VentureBeat, 2026; The Next Web in the Canva case file, 2026). Fast Company (2026) describes a "frenemies" market in which Canva is a close partner, Figma and Adobe are rivals under pressure, and both position themselves as AI-agnostic. Figma's May 2026 10-Q adds a risk factor that model providers "may at the same time offer competing products, seek to intermediate end-user relationships directly" (Figma filing in the Figma case file, 2026). This is coopetition in the Brandenburger and Nalebuff sense: Canva is simultaneously a complementor (the editing and deployment layer behind the agent) and a substitute (the user may never open Canva if Claude Design suffices). The theory gives no tidy prediction on whether the complementor role is stable. Brandenburger and Stuart's added-value logic suggests the lab will keep a partner only while the partner adds value the lab cannot cheaply replicate, which turns attention to Section 2.6.

### 2.6 Which incumbent assets should resist, and which should not

Combining the barrier matrix, the platform results and the case files gives the following reading. I rank by how well the theory and the evidence support resistance; none is established empirically for SaaS creation tools.

| Asset | Theory | Predicted resistance | Case anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Distribution and installed base (hundreds of millions of users, defaults in suites) | Zhu and Iansiti 2012; Tunguz 2023; envelopment | Medium to high, but it also helps the suite owners (Google, Microsoft) against the incumbents | Canva's user base; Google Pics inside Workspace |
| Collaboration and shared libraries (network effects) | Farrell and Klemperer 2007 | High where multi-user, weaker for single-user creation | Figma multiplayer files; less for Grammarly's single-user overlay |
| File formats and editable structure | Switching costs; Klemperer 1995 | Medium: agents convert files, but editable layers have value | Canva as "final mile" of editing; Figma components and auto layout |
| Templates, brand kits, design systems (customer-specific complementary assets) | Teece 1986; Tripsas 1997 (register); Azoulay et al. 2025 | Medium to high if the incumbent serves them to agents on its terms (S4); low if the lab can rebuild them from code and files | Claude Design builds a design system from codebase and files |
| Brand and trust, governance (indemnity, admin controls) | Differentiation barrier; Bain 1956 | Medium, strongest in enterprise | Canva Shield; Grammarly authorship tools |
| Proprietary data | Hagiu and Wright 2023; Casado and Lauten 2019 | Low to medium: weaker than assumed | Brand data matters only if portable access is restricted |
| Model quality or the AI feature itself | Barney and Reeves 2024 | Low: commodity input | All cases use third-party models, partly replaced by in-house ones |
| The pre-AI skill barrier (Canva's "anyone can design", Grammarly's correction) | Adner and Zemsky 2006 | Low: general assistants deliver the same promise | Writing and image generation by general assistants |

Two assets deserve a note because they cut both ways. Brand kits and design systems are complementary assets, but they are also information that an agent can read and, in principle, reconstruct; this is why the terms under which incumbents serve context (S4) become the real battleground. Template libraries, by contrast, are less protected because generation can create layouts on demand, a prediction that fits the observation in the Canva case file that Canva's agent is taught to adapt library designs rather than generate new ones.

### 2.7 The practitioner "moat" debate versus the academic view

Practitioners frame the debate as "moats": data (a16z, 2019, later qualified), distribution (Tunguz; LSVP), workflow depth and context (Foundation Capital), and speed of shipping. Investors disagree on who wins. The 2023 a16z stack essay expected infrastructure vendors to capture most value (Bornstein, Appenzeller and Casado, 2023), and Menlo's 2025 enterprise survey reports that more than half of USD 37B in enterprise AI spend went to applications and that the share of purchased rather than built AI solutions rose (Menlo Ventures, 2025; figures via secondary summaries). Elad Gil writes that mobile value went to incumbents while this wave will contain strong startup success (Gil, 2023). The academic literature is more cautious: it supports "complementary assets" as the moat (Azoulay et al., 2025), questions data as a moat (Hagiu and Wright, 2023), and says very little about SaaS creation tools specifically. Two regularities appear across sources: practitioners date the fastest growth to AI-native entrants but also report thin margins and unproven retention (Bessemer, 2025; RevenueCat coverage), and almost all essays were written before the 2026 model-lab launches (Claude Design, Google Pics) and do not model them.

### 2.8 What is established, contested and missing

**Established (with the caveats of Section 1).** Entry in software-related ventures rose after ChatGPT and the new firms are smaller (four independent working papers, direction consistent). AI-native firms report faster revenue ramps than earlier SaaS cohorts. Platform owners enter complementors' markets and complementors adjust. Complementary assets, not model technology, are the favored moat in the academic account.

**Contested.** Whether build cost has fallen for production software (METR and Bain versus the "vibe coding" cases). Whether AI-native growth is durable (retention and margin). Whether incumbents' distribution protects them or only protects the suite owners. Whether serving context to external agents (S4 with S2) strengthens or leaks the incumbent's assets. Whether coopetition with model labs is stable.

**Missing.** (1) No peer-reviewed study of incumbents' strategic responses to generative AI in creation SaaS (design, presentation, writing). (2) No study that measures substitution of Canva, Figma or Grammarly by general assistants; the substitution evidence concerns Stack Overflow (Burtch, Lee and Chen, 2024; del Rio-Chanona et al., 2024) and freelance markets (Hui, Reshef and Zhou, 2023; Demirci, Hannane and Zhu, 2024), which are adjacent. (3) No empirical test of the claim that design-system or brand-kit context resists substitution. (4) The inference-cost twist to the digital-economics baseline (Goldfarb and Tucker, 2019) is not integrated in the entry literature. (5) Model labs as simultaneous supplier, complementor and rival are treated in platform theory but not for agentic use of third-party tools.

## 3. Anchoring to the cases

Evidence below comes from `lavoro/casi/*.md`; it is not re-verified here.

**Gamma (AI-native benchmark).** Founded in late 2020 with a card-based presentation tool, pivoted in March 2023 to AI generation using GPT-4, reports USD 100M ARR, profit, about 50 employees and 70M users (all self-reported, TechCrunch, 2025). It fits the entry-evidence pattern (small team, fast ramp: Stripe, 2025; Bessemer, 2025; Kim et al., 2026) and the "wrapper" pattern, since it uses third-party models and shows the risk of upstream suppliers entering (Gupta and Garg, 2025; Korinek and Vipra, 2024). Its API, MCP and connectors in Claude and ChatGPT are S2 moves of an entrant; its partner-agent role in Superhuman Go is S3 from the other side. The literature does not explain why Gamma has kept its position against Copilot-like bundling, a point to test in interviews or filings; it does predict that Gamma's durability depends on assets beyond the model (Barney and Reeves, 2024).

**Figma.** Figma's files are structured (components, variables, auto layout) and its multi-user canvas adds network effects, so the theory predicts relatively strong resistance (Farrell and Klemperer, 2007; Azoulay et al., 2025). The case file records the opposite risk too: Claude Design builds a design system from codebase and design files and offers no Figma export, and the 10-Q adds a model-provider risk factor. This is platform-owner entry (Gawer and Henderson, 2007) with the added feature that the entrant is also a supplier of Figma's own models. Figma's moves (own agent, MCP for external agents, design-system context, first-party models, scope toward code) correspond to the responses the literature suggests: serve context on own terms (S4), pull the agent inside (S1), diversify (S6). The literature cannot say whether the MCP seat anchor survives, because it does not treat agents as "users" for pricing.

**Canva.** Canva's original barrier was the skill barrier (design for non-designers), the one generative AI erodes most. Canva's counter-assets are the installed base, templates, brand kits and the editable-artifact role. The case file shows it acting as complementor and rival of Claude Design at once (design back end and "export to Canva"), which is the coopetition scenario of Brandenburger and Nalebuff (1996). The theory predicts instability: Canva keeps its role while it adds value the lab cannot replicate, and the move to its own models (cost and editability) is consistent with reducing dependence. Eisenmann et al. (2011) suggests another threat, envelopment by suites, which the Google Pics launch represents.

**Adobe Express.** Adobe has a large professional asset base (file formats, Firefly, Creative Cloud) and a platform position, which the theory classes as strong resistance to entry (Lieberman and Montgomery, 1998; Bain, 1956), but Express competes for the non-designer segment where the skill barrier has fallen. Google Pics bundled in Workspace is the textbook envelopment case; the literature predicts incumbents with their own bundles (Adobe, Microsoft) resist better than stand-alone tools.

**Grammarly (Superhuman).** The pre-AI barrier was a linguistic model plus presence at the cursor; general assistants produce and rewrite text directly (Noy and Zhang, 2023; Hui, Reshef and Zhou, 2023), so substitution is the main threat (Adner and Zemsky, 2006). The case file shows scope expansion to a suite and hosting of third-party agents (S3, S6, S7). The literature supports the logic of leaving a commoditized core, and Mobility-barrier theory (Caves and Porter, 1977) warns that moves between groups are costly; the case file's view that scope moves partly leave the creation category is consistent with that.

## 4. Implications for the thesis

**Propositions the literature supports (with strength).**
1. Entry barriers related to build cost and skill have fallen for small artifacts (moderate, preprint-level evidence: Kim et al., 2026; Cao and Bhatia, 2025; Marchesi and Tang, 2025; Cai and Gu, 2025).
2. The durable assets of an incumbent are complementary and customer-specific (distribution, collaboration, brand kits, design systems), not technology (Azoulay et al., 2025; Teece, 1986 in the register).
3. Model labs behave as platform owners entering complementors' markets, and the relationship is coopetition (Gawer and Henderson, 2007; Brandenburger and Nalebuff, 1996; Fast Company, 2026).
4. Whether the incumbent's agent is a point or a system solution decides who wins (Agrawal, Gans and Goldfarb, 2022).

**Propositions the literature challenges.** (a) "Data is the moat" (Hagiu and Wright, 2023; Casado and Lauten, 2019), which weakens a naive reading of S4; (b) "software is now cheap to build" in general (METR, 2025; Bain, 2025); (c) "installed base alone protects" (Zhu and Iansiti, 2012).

**Strategies informed.**
- S1 (own agent): point solution; defends but does not by itself create a moat (Barney and Reeves, 2024).
- S2 (used by external agents): coopetition and envelopment risk; the added-value logic suggests terms must keep the incumbent's added value visible (Brandenburger and Stuart, 1996).
- S3 (hosting third-party agents): platform logic; relevant for Grammarly more than for Figma and Canva.
- S4 (serving context): the theory says this is the core of defense but it also leaks the asset; terms of access are the research object.
- S5 (cost-driven monetization): Goldfarb and Tucker (2019) assume near-zero marginal cost; inference breaks that.
- S6 (scope): mobility barriers (Caves and Porter, 1977) predict difficulty; entrants cross them cheaply.
- S7 (acquisition): Base44 to Wix (Globes, 2025) and the case files show that incumbents buy entrants, which fits Ansari et al. (2016) on absorbing the disruptor.
- Rival hypotheses: governance as a lever finds only weak support here (security and accountability concerns in Veracode, 2025, and a model of openness and integration in Xu et al., 2025); outcome-based pricing is outside this stream's coverage.

**Gaps the thesis can claim.** (1) Applying entry-barrier and platform-entry theory to creation-oriented SaaS facing model-lab products, with five dated cases; (2) specifying which incumbent assets are served to agents, on which terms, and what that implies for added value; (3) linking the barrier matrix (Table 1) to observed moves in the case files; (4) measuring, via the case evidence, how inference cost changes the digital-economics baseline.

**Caution on evidence.** The empirical entry papers and most grey sources were read through summaries only. The thesis should either open them or state explicitly that figures are taken from secondary reports.
