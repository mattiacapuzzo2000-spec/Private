## 2. Thematic synthesis

### 2.1 What was actually said, and by whom

The vocabulary of the debate rests on three events that are repeated more often than they are checked.

*Nadella on BG2 (12 December 2024).* The most quoted passage is reliable in two independent transcripts and in a third that reproduces it (OfficeChai; CX Today, 23 December 2024; The Register, 2026; SP001-SP003). Nadella said that business applications are "essentially CRUD databases with a bunch of business logic", that the logic "is all going to these AI agents", that agents will be "multi-repo CRUD" and not discriminate between back ends, and that "once the AI tier becomes the place where all the logic is, then people will start replacing the back ends". Two refinements matter for the thesis. First, the claim is about the back end and logic tier of business applications (Dynamics, finance and operations), where Microsoft said it would "go pretty aggressively and try and collapse it all"; CX Today notes that the Excel example in the same answer describes transformation, with Copilot using Excel "as a tool with all of its action space". Second, he asked "why do I need Excel?" in the same breath: it is the only explicit statement by a major CEO on a creation and productivity tool, and it is posed as a thought experiment, not a forecast. The short line "RIP to all software related jobs" circulating with it is from a Reddit post (grey_notes Part D item 7); but the phrase "go pretty aggressively and try and collapse it all" is genuine, so the earlier note should be narrowed, not extended to the whole sentence.

*Klarna (September 2024 and March 2025).* In an analyst call the CEO said "We just shut down Salesforce. Within a few weeks, we will shut down Workday" (SP005). It was read as AI replacing SaaS. Six months later he stated that Klarna "did not replace SaaS with an LLM"; it consolidated data from many SaaS tools onto an internal knowledge stack (Neo4j and other components), used Cursor to build new interfaces, and expected "fewer SaaS" to consolidate the market (Bort, 2025, SP004). Benioff's immediate reply was about governance, compliance and institutional memory; Workday called replacement "a specious misunderstanding" (SP005). HBR still cites Klarna as an example of exit from SaaS (Nishar & Nohria, 2025, SP040). The Klarna episode is thus a case of build-versus-buy consolidation, with AI lowering interface-building costs, not of an LLM substituting a system of record.

*The coinage.* Bloomberg's report of 3 February 2026 quotes Jeffrey Favuzza, who works on the equity trading desk at Jefferies: "We call it the 'SaaSpocalypse,' an apocalypse for software-as-a-service stocks. Trading is very much 'get me out' style selling" (SP006, V-open through a verbatim repost). This upgrades the earlier [V-snippet] attribution (G40). The label therefore describes a trading condition coined by a trader, not an analytical claim, and later analysts used it as a foil ("Beyond the SaaSpocalypse", Forrester, SP017; "Catchy Title, Flawed Premise", IDC, G05). The same Bloomberg text records the context: the S&P North American software index fell 15% in January 2026, its biggest monthly decline since October 2008, and only 71% of S&P 500 software companies beat revenue expectations against 85% for tech overall.

### 2.2 A typology of narratives

The positions in the corpus sort into six narratives, plus one cross-cutting thread (the cost of AI). They are not mutually exclusive: several actors hold two.

| Code | Narrative | Core claim | Main holders (sources) | Type of evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| N1 | Replacement | Apps and back ends are replaced by an agent logic tier or by in-house builds | Nadella 2024 (SP001-002); Klarna's first reading (SP005); Bessemer on systems of record (SP029); Nishar & Nohria (SP040); Cao (SP044); Saha (SP046); IDC's long-run "agents replacing entire applications" (G06) | Executive statements, conceptual papers, three anecdotal cases; no measured replacement |
| N2 | Seat cannibalisation | Agents reduce the number of paid users, breaking the link between customers' value and vendor revenue | Gartner (35% of point products; 40% of spend; USD 234 billion by 2030) (SP011-012); McKinsey (SP014); Activant (SP034); Bain pricing (SP019, SP020); Forrester (SP016, SP018); EY survey (SP025); Deloitte (G08); BCG (SP022); Schroders in G20 | Analyst forecasts, expectations surveys, a few vendor cases (GitLab); seat counts mostly still rising |
| N3 | Intermediation by agents | Agents become the front end; applications turn into APIs and data services whose UI no longer protects them | a16z (SP031-032); Gartner "agentic arbitrage" (SP011); Goldman's "orchestration layer" (SP027); Forrester control planes (SP016); Bain's three-layer stack (G01); IDC "if an agent cannot find your capability" (G05); Klein & Wieczorek (SP045); Sahni on readability (SP047) | Conceptual and investor essays; one descriptive audit; incumbent actions (Salesforce "headless", April 2026) |

Three observations follow. (i) The strong claims (N1) have the weakest evidence and are mostly held by actors who sell agents, platforms, compute or venture portfolios. (ii) The best-evidenced narrative about vendors' economics is the hybrid one: seats are weakening as a pricing unit but not collapsing, and consumption or outcome elements are being layered on (SP019, SP022, SP030, SP054). (iii) The typology omits what the sources rarely say: who benefits when a creation tool's output is produced by someone else's agent (section 2.8).

### 2.3 The sell-off as an event: what is established

The sequence, from sources opened this session and the existing register, is: Cowork research preview 12 January 2026; Cowork plugins 30 January; the 3 February sell-off (Thomson Reuters about -16% at the close, RELX -14%; G20, G21); the label in Bloomberg the same day (SP006); a week of losses in the S&P software and services index of nearly 5%, about USD 830 billion on Reuters' count (G22); Bain's note of 9 February reading the move as a reset in confidence while "installed bases have largely held up, with average gross retention still around 90% or better" (SP020); a trough around 10 to 13 April; Claude Design on 17 April (G26, G27); a 41% rebound of the software ETF between 13 April and 12 October (G24).

Three points are contested or fragile. *Magnitude.* Published tallies are not comparable and must not be combined: more than USD 1 trillion in seven days (Forrester, SP016), USD 830 billion in a week (Reuters, G22), USD 730 billion in a month for six vendors (The Register, SP003), USD 285 to 300 billion in two days across software and data indices (SP039, snippets), about USD 2 trillion since the September 2025 peak (SaaStr; BCG, SP038, SP022). *Cause.* The event was triggered by a general-purpose agent with role plugins and hit data and legal providers first (Morningstar's comment that the legal plugin "has nothing to do with legal research", G21). One reading attributes the move to margin compression, private-credit exposure and platform fatigue that predate Anthropic (Schuler, SP039); the January index fall had already occurred. Software forward earnings multiples fell from 84x (2020-22) to 22.7x in early 2026, below the S&P 500 (SaaStr, SP038, Bloomberg data per the author). *Persistence.* Goldman's positioning data show hedge funds' software weight at its lowest since 2019 as of May 2026 (SP026), yet the sector rebounded strongly by October (G24) and BCG states that "much of that decline has since reversed" (SP022).

What is not available is a measurement of causal impact. The only event-study paper retrieved concerns AI winners, not SaaS (Bhushan, 2026, SP051). A proper study (market-model abnormal returns around 30 January, 3 February and 17 April, cross-sectioned by seat exposure, switching costs and category) is feasible and absent. Stanton's HBR analysis of 45 public SaaS companies, classified by task type (deterministic versus predictive) and data type (internal versus pooled), is the closest thing: it reports (snippet) that predictive, pooled-data vendors fell about 4% in the sell-off against 9% for deterministic, internal-data vendors (SP041). It is a practitioner article by a professor, not a reviewed study, and its details need the full text.

### 2.4 Analysts and consultancies: convergence and divergence

*Convergence.* Every major analyst and consultancy in the corpus rejects the simple death thesis and accepts that seat-based pricing is under pressure: Bain ("Disruption is mandatory; obsolescence is not", SP020; "seats are dead" is "an overstatement", SP019), Forrester ("death of the core... overstated", SP016), IDC (G05), Gartner (headline USD 234 billion at risk, about 20% of enterprise SaaS spend, SP011, but not total collapse), McKinsey (agents as users of SaaS or agent-centric architectures, SP014, snippet), Goldman (market grows to USD 780 billion by 2030 with value aggregating at the orchestration layer, SP027, snippet), BCG (SP022). Hybrid pricing is the agreed interim state (Bain, BCG, ICONIQ, Microsoft: SP019, SP022, SP030, SP054).

*Divergence 1: which layer is safe.* Bain, Forrester and IDC say the systems of record are protected by switching costs, criticality, data and compliance ("for most enterprise systems of record those moats remain strong", SP020). Bessemer and a16z say the opposite: the moat of a system of record was the human interface and the cost of migration, both of which AI erodes ("those moats are degrading", SP029; "the UI was the product", SP031; the CRM becomes "one of many inputs", SP032). Salesforce's own April 2026 "headless" launch, which a16z calls a repositioning of APIs that "have largely existed for years", suggests the incumbent accepts the investors' premise while the analysts do not. Investor portfolios are in AI-natives, so the divergence is also one of interest.

*Divergence 2: timing.* Gartner dates the impact to 2030; Deloitte says replacing apps with agents will take "at least five years" (G08); Forrester's 2027 predictions expect fewer than 10% of enterprises to run autonomous cross-app agents (SP018); IDC projects agents replacing entire applications in the early 2030s (G06); the Microsoft-linked Rothschild et al. argue that disruption has "not fully arrived" because organisations use AI to speed pre-AI workflows (SP043). Venture sources describe the change as already under way.

*Divergence 3: who captures value.* Foundation Capital and Sequoia see the next large companies selling work, not tools (SP035, G13); Bain 2026 sizes a USD 100 billion US opportunity in cross-system labour (G03); Forrester warns that AI consumption pricing will "backfire" for three large app vendors because customers will build agents in platforms already licensed (SP018); Gartner says the decisive clause in future contracts is who owns what the system learns (SP011).

*Interest.* Consultancies sell transformation and pricing advice; BCG's 2026 article is a collaboration with Stripe and AWS, which benefit from usage billing (SP022); EY's survey subset is AI-investing leaders (SP025); Gartner addresses buyers (CIOs) and emphasises risk; venture firms hold AI-native stakes (Bessemer, a16z, Foundation, Activant, 8VC); HBR's Nishar is an investor in Anthropic and Figma (SP040). No source in the corpus is disinterested.

### 2.5 Evidence on fundamentals: revenue, seats and buyers

The fundamentals through mid-2026 favour N5 for enterprise vendors. ServiceNow's subscription growth rose for five consecutive quarters to 23%, cRPO grew 21.5%, AI annual contract value passed USD 1 billion and renewal held at 98%; SAP's cloud revenue grew 24% and its CFO called the debate "massive noise" (SP010). Microsoft reported over 30 million paid Microsoft 365 Copilot seats while paid commercial seats grew 6% (SP054, snippet). Aggregate software spending was forecast to grow about 15% in 2026 (Gartner, SP013, snippet). Forrester projects SaaS spend rising from USD 318 billion (2025) to USD 576 billion (2029) (SP016). Salesforce says seats in Agentforce Sales, Service and Slack rose year on year, though its cRPO disappointed and the stock fell (SP008). On the buyer side, Oliver Wyman's survey of more than 150 North American buyers finds few expecting seat counts to fall faster than before (SP023), whereas EY finds 82% of AI-investing senior leaders expect per-seat pricing to matter less within five years (SP025). The two are compatible: near-term seat counts are stable while expectations about the pricing logic shift.

The counter-signals are about growth quality and distribution of value, not revenue collapse. Bain reports that growth has halved from about 20% and net revenue retention has fallen about nine points since 2021 (SP021), and that buyers' budgets are moving to AI tooling (SP020). Activant argues that seats do not capture value: GitLab's deployments per user rose 35-45% while it posted its slowest growth and 118% NRR (SP034). Gartner's Brocklehurst warns that stable SaaS revenue is not a stable SaaS business model (as reported in the ServiceNow coverage, snippet). The reading best supported is therefore *repricing and slower growth before substitution*.

### 2.6 Academic and quasi-academic work: thin, conceptual, unreviewed

The peer-reviewed literature on this question is almost empty for 2025-2026. What exists falls in four groups.

*Conceptual preprints on agentic software and the firm.* Cao (2026, SP044) describes licensed software, SaaS and agent-as-a-service as successive transfers of complexity, ending with "the agent itself is the software". Klein and Wieczorek (2026, SP045) model coordination cost: protocol-mediated agents reduce integration cost from quadratic to linear and select an "hourglass" firm with a generative interface on top, a protocol waist and competing execution agents below. Rothschild et al. (2026, SP043) offer an augmentation, automation, reconstruction lens in which the large effects need complementary investments (trust infrastructure, machine-legible data, new workflows). These sit with the register's NEW02-NEW05 and give theoretical content to N3.

*Pricing and economics.* Zhang (2026, SP049) models the mismatch between upstream token pricing and downstream subscription pricing; Zhao (2026, SP050) proposes multi-part "job envelope" tariffs; Tagliapietra Sanabria (2026, SP048) argues SaaS "regresses to infrastructure" under an outcome-capture principle. Nothing here tests the claims. The register already holds the pricing items NEW09-NEW12.

*Empirical fragments.* Sahni (2026, SP047) audits 274 B2B software homepages for agent readability (llms.txt, structured data, crawler rules): productivity and collaboration software has a median score of 55.5 against 93 for sales and marketing software, and blocks AI crawlers most often. Lenz (2026, SP058) shows for banking that effort-based switching costs explain a modest share of rate-driven switching and that agents may push that cost to zero, a mechanism that transfers to software. Saha (2026, SP046) offers three anecdotal rebuilds. None is reviewed.

*Practitioner-academic outlets.* HBR (Nishar & Nohria, 2025; Stanton, 2026) and CMR (Li, Qu & Divekar, 2026: a general review, SP042) are the only business-school-linked outlets found. MIT SMR returned nothing relevant.

The honest conclusion for the methodology chapter is that the debate is conducted almost entirely in grey literature, and that the grey sources are mostly forecasts and arguments by interested parties, with some usable company disclosures and surveys. The established theoretical lenses in the academic register (Teece, 1986; Tripsas, 1997; Christensen, 1997; ecosystem and platform theory) have not been applied to the SaaSpocalypse in any retrieved paper: that is Stream L2's task, and a gap for L4.

### 2.7 Counter-arguments and their limits

*Systems of record, data gravity, switching costs.* This is the dominant incumbent and analyst defence (Benioff, SP005, SP008; Workday, SP005; Bain, SP020; Forrester, SP016; 8VC, SP033; Huang, SP009). It is empirically supported by 2025-26 revenue and retention (SP010, SP020) and logically by the cost of migrating records and compliance. Its limits are four. It protects records, not interfaces or point tools; Forrester itself predicts horizontal point-solution vendors with low switching costs will be challenged (SP016) and Gartner expects 35% of point products to be replaced or absorbed (SP012). It is weakened where AI lowers migration cost (Bessemer, SP029; Lenz, SP058). It does not protect against loss of the front end to an agent (N3). And it is advanced mostly by the firms that hold the records.

*"SaaS will evolve, not die."* Accepted by almost everyone, which makes it close to unfalsifiable; the substantive question is who captures value after the evolution. Ball puts it as "overestimating in the short term, underestimating in the long term" (SP037).

*Build-your-own.* Ball rejects it (total cost of ownership, speed, focus, maintenance, scale, compliance) but argues the real risk is that "the cost of creating software is going to zero" for entrants who design for agents and consumption pricing (SP037). 8VC agrees that "code replicability was never the moat" (SP033). The supervisor's framing ("anyone can do it") is therefore best read as a barrier-to-entry argument, not a customer-substitution argument. Klarna, the most cited case of build, is a consolidation case (SP004).

*Non-AI explanations.* Margin compression, post-2021 growth normalisation, private-credit exposure and rate effects are offered as alternative or co-causes (Schuler, SP039; SaaStr, SP038; Bain, SP021).

### 2.8 What the debate says, and does not say, about creation and productivity tools

The literature on the SaaSpocalypse is overwhelmingly about enterprise systems of record (CRM, ERP, HCM, ITSM, support). Checked directly: Bain's 2025 Technology Report mentions Adobe only in a list of examples of AI inside workflows ("writing ad copy in Adobe's Experience Cloud", p. 22) and analyses CRM, support, marketing automation and similar workflows; IDC's markets, per the existing register, include no design or document tools; Gartner's 2026 study speaks of "enterprise application" spending and of buying software "for agents" (SP011); McKinsey's archetypes are enterprise (SP014, snippet). Creation tools enter the debate in five ways only.

1. *CEO remarks.* Nadella's "why do I need Excel?" (SP002) and Huang's counter that agents will use Excel and browsers rather than rewrite them (SP009, snippet) are the two poles: the agent as replacement of the tool versus the agent as user of the tool. Both are about Excel, a creation tool, and both pre-date any measurement.
2. *Lab products hitting the stock.* Claude Design and Figma, Adobe and Wix shares (G26, G27; SP053, SP055). Figma raised guidance weeks later (SP055, snippet), the market-versus-fundamentals gap seen in enterprise names.
3. *Investor worry about seats.* Fortune links Adobe's falling shares, despite record revenue of USD 6.40 billion, to fear that agents "could weaken demand for parts of the traditional seat-based software model" (SP053). No seat data are shown.
4. *Forrester's disruption model* lists "creative services" among the most disrupted markets (SP017), but these are services labour markets (agencies, production), not creation software; the category placement of design and document tools is behind the paywall.
5. *Machine legibility.* Sahni's audit finds productivity and collaboration software least prepared for agent reading (SP047), and a16z argues that when "humans lived in the interface" the interface was the moat (SP031).

What the debate does not say is more important. (a) *It has no theory of the artifact.* In a system of record, the product is a record of truth that agents must read and write; in a creation tool, the product is an artifact (deck, design, document) that an agent can produce without the tool's interface, and the tool's value lies in the editor, collaboration, templates, assets, brand kits, file formats and the network of collaborators. The a16z logic that the UI was the product (SP031) therefore applies with more force to creation tools than to CRMs, while the data-gravity defence applies with less. This is an inference to be tested, not a finding of the literature. (b) *Seat logic differs.* Seats in enterprise systems follow headcount in the automated function; in creation tools, falling production cost may increase the number of creators and artifacts (Canva reports that users of its AI create three times as many designs, SP052). Whether this offsets seat compression is not examined by any source. (c) *Outcome pricing is hard to apply* to artifacts whose quality is subjective; the outcome-pricing evidence is from support, sales and IT tickets (SP019, SP012, G09). (d) *The cost-of-AI thread is strongest in creation tools,* where generation is the product and each artifact consumes inference: Canva cut its 2026 growth outlook by a third (SP052), and Fortune links Figma's shrinking margins to the same problem. (e) *Distribution and brand are never analysed as moats* except in passing ("taste, brand, customer success, trust", SP033; Dylan Field's "design is the differentiator", SP055). The Stanton matrix could classify creation tools by task and data type, but the classification has not been done.

### 2.9 The cost-of-AI thread

Added to the typology because the SQ3 of the thesis depends on it. Bain states that "the compute required to power AI adds substantial costs to every query, shifting revenue models from seat-based subscriptions toward usage and outcomes" (SP021); PitchBook's analyst says "AI is making SaaS no longer a zero marginal cost solution" (SP052); Zhang models a vertical pricing mismatch between token-priced inputs and subscription-priced outputs (SP049); EY finds 98% of token-using leaders have reconsidered their approach (SP025); Forrester predicts that metering AI per action will backfire when customers build agents in suites they already license (SP018). The threat is therefore not only external (replacement, seat loss) but internal (variable cost, margin), and the incumbent's response (credits, rationing) risks a customer response (bundle arbitrage).

## 3. Anchoring to the cases

## 4. Implications for the thesis

**Propositions supported.** (P1) AI has lowered the barrier to producing software and artifacts, and incumbents' accumulated assets (installed base, formats, collaboration, brand, data, compliance) are the defensible part: supported conceptually by Ball (SP037), 8VC (SP033), Bain (SP020) and Teece-type reasoning; direct evidence for creation tools is thin. (P2) Seat-based pricing is weakening as the sole unit and is being replaced by hybrids: supported by Bain, BCG, ICONIQ, Microsoft (SP019, SP022, SP030, SP054). (P3) The cost of AI is a first-order strategic variable for creation incumbents: supported by SP021, SP049, SP052. (P4) Access terms and context are contested resources: supported by Gartner (SP011), a16z (SP031) and Bain's semantic-layer argument (G01).

**Propositions challenged.** The strong replacement claim that agents will soon replace creation tools is not supported by any measurement; the sell-off was a repricing, revenue held for enterprise leaders, and corrections to the Klarna and Nadella readings narrow the evidence for N1. The claim that all SaaS is uniformly threatened is challenged by Forrester (SP017), Stanton (SP041) and Bain (SP020). The assumption that outcome-based pricing will be the main destination is challenged by the dominance of hybrids (SP019, SP030) and is unproven for artifacts.

**Strategies informed.** S1 (own agent): McKinsey's archetypes, Bain's "launch their own agents fast", Benioff's agentic enterprise (SP014, G01, SP007). S2 (used by external agents under terms): Gartner's contract warning and the "buy for agents" framing (SP011), Salesforce "headless" (SP031), Huang's tool-user view (SP009), Sahni's readability audit (SP047), IDC (G05). S3 (hosting third-party agents): Goldman's orchestration layer (SP027), Bain's neutral platform option (G01), Klein & Wieczorek's protocol waist (SP045). S4 (proprietary context): Gartner's Knowledge Retention Rate (SP011), Bain's decision context (G03), 8VC's three tiers (SP033), McKinsey's expert agents (SP014). S5 (hybrid monetisation): SP019, SP022, SP030, SP021; Forrester warns of backfire (SP018); Fortune/Canva (SP052). S6 (scope): consolidation expectations (SP004, SP016). S7 (acquisition): Forrester's prediction that niche AI-natives get absorbed (SP016), Bessemer's "incumbents strike back as AI M&A heats up" (SP029, table of contents). R-outcome: SP035, G13, G03, SP048 for; SP019, SP030 against as yet. R-governance: Benioff and Workday on compliance (SP005), Gartner on contract and learning ownership (SP011), Forrester on sovereignty and control planes (SP018), Rothschild et al. on trust infrastructure (SP043).

**Gaps the thesis can claim.** (G-a) *Creation SaaS is absent from every analyst framework and from the academic work:* the grey literature covers systems of record, support and sales; Forrester's creative category is services. (G-b) *No event study or other causal test of the SaaS sell-off exists;* a note in the methodology chapter can offer one for the five cases' listed peers (Adobe, Figma, Wix, Atlassian) around 30 January, 3 February and 17 April. (G-c) *No study of vendors' actual terms of access for external agents* (API terms, MCP policies, credit rules), though Gartner identifies terms as a risk: the cases supply exactly this evidence. (G-d) *Artifact economics:* the effect of falling production cost on the number of creators, artifacts and seats in creation tools is unexamined. (G-e) *No application of incumbent-adaptation theory (complementary assets, ambidexterity, business model innovation) to SaaS and agents* in any retrieved paper. (G-f) *Business model change versus feature change* (SQ2) is never distinguished in practitioner sources: "pricing change" is treated as the whole of the business model.
