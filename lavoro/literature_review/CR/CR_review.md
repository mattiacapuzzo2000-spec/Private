## 2. Thematic synthesis

### 2.1 What is "productivity software" and what is "creation software"? Definitions and taxonomies

The academic definitions come from two traditions that rarely meet. The first is end-user computing and end-user software engineering, which treats the people who build artifacts without being professional developers as the unit of analysis (Ko et al., 2011): most programs, spreadsheets and, by extension, documents and designs are made by people whose expertise lies elsewhere, and they face the same engineering problems as professionals. The second is the creativity support tool (CST) tradition in human-computer interaction. Shneiderman (2007) frames CSTs through one question, how to let "more people be more creative more often", and the 2006 NSF workshop report (Shneiderman et al., 2006) set the agenda. Later reviews show a field that is technology-centred and weak on shared definitions and on evaluation (Frich et al., 2019; Remy et al., 2020; Cox et al., 2025). None of these papers defines "productivity SaaS". The CST literature measures creative outcome, experience and agency; the office-productivity tradition measures time and throughput. This matters for the thesis: the five cases sit in the first tradition by origin (Figma and Canva as CSTs, Grammarly as an assistive writing tool) but are sold as the second (seats, teams, enterprise productivity).

Industry taxonomies confirm the split. Gartner's collaborative work management category is defined around task-driven workspaces to plan, coordinate and automate work, and Gartner places Notion in a separate "collaborative content workspace" bucket (CR032; snippet-level evidence from secondary summaries). IDC's software taxonomy lists collaborative applications, content workflow and management applications and "creative applications" as separate functional markets (CR033; snippet level). I found no analyst category called "content creation". Competition authorities, by contrast, define markets by what the tool does: the CMA's provisional findings speak of "all-in-one product design software for professional users", vector editing and raster editing (CR025), and the Commission speaks of "interactive product design software" and "other creative design software", with raster, vector and video as "digital assets" (CR027). Figma itself defines its market by people, not by artifact: a USD 33 bn total addressable market built on an IDC study, commissioned by Figma, of the "global workforce engaged in software design" (CR030). Three definitions, three logics (function, artifact type, workforce). The thesis should say which it adopts, and why "creation-oriented productivity SaaS" is a population defined by the artifact produced (a design, a deck, a document, a text) and by the job of the product (to make an artifact), not by an analyst category. This is a gap the thesis can state clearly (section 4).

### 2.2 The business models of creative software: subscription, freemium, product-led growth

What is established. Adobe's current model is subscription-dominant: subscription revenue is 96% of total revenue in fiscal 2025, Digital Media ARR is USD 19.20 bn (+11.5%) and Digital Media revenue USD 17.65 bn (Adobe 10-K, CR029). The Commission's referral decision describes Adobe's products as "sold on a subscription basis, either as stand-alone products or as part of Adobe's 'Creative Cloud' product suite" (CR028, para 10), and Figma as a web-based tool with a free Starter tier and paid Professional, Organization and Enterprise tiers (para 12). Figma describes its growth as "product-led, bottoms-up adoption" supported by direct sales (CR030). Canva's and Gamma's models, per the case files, are freemium plus subscription, now with credits.

What is thin. I did not find academic analysis of Adobe's move to Creative Cloud, which is surprising given how often it is invoked as the archetype of a licence-to-subscription transition. Blog accounts exist (for example on a drop in average transaction value and on deferred revenue), but I could not attribute them to a recognisable author or data source, and I excluded them. The freemium literature is general (Niculescu and Wu, 2014, on free tiers under perpetual licensing; Gu et al., 2018, on conversion to premium; both V-meta only) and does not treat template economies, creator marketplaces or the effect of AI credits on the free tier. Canva's template marketplace and creator-compensation scheme are documented only in trade press and company statements (CR047), not in scholarly work. This is a real gap for S5 (hybrid monetization) and S4 (proprietary context), and I flag it as one of the gaps the thesis can claim, with the caveat that the claim rests on my search and that the student must repeat it in HBS and Emerald catalogues.

What practitioners and academics disagree on is not yet visible here; the gap is that there is nothing to disagree about. The pricing literature already in the registers (Saltan and Smolander, 2021; the AI pricing preprints NEW09 to NEW12) is where S5 theory must come from.

### 2.3 Competition and market structure: the Adobe/Figma decisions as primary evidence

The abandoned Adobe/Figma merger is the richest primary source in this stream and is, in a sense, the only place where a public authority wrote down how the design software market works. The Commission accepted the Article 22 referral on 14 February 2023 after requests from Austria and 15 other authorities (CR028; deal value USD 20 bn, half cash and half stock, signed 15 September 2022). On 17 November 2023 it issued a Statement of Objections (CR027), with preliminary views on two theories of harm: (1) in interactive product design Figma is "the clear market leader and Adobe one of its largest competitors", and discontinuing Adobe XD would be a "reverse killer acquisition"; (2) in vector and raster editing, Figma "already exerts a significant constraining influence" on Illustrator and Photoshop and is "significantly likely to enter these markets and grow into an effective competitive force" (potential competition). The CMA's provisional findings of 28 November 2023 are parallel: substantial lessening of competition in the global supply of all-in-one product design software for professional users, vector editing and raster editing (CR025); the press release adds that around 80% of the professional product design market use Figma, that Adobe abandoned a new product design tool because of the merger, and that Figma's threat had driven development of Photoshop and Illustrator web versions (CR026). The deal was terminated in December 2023; Figma received a USD 1.0 bn termination fee (CR030) and Adobe booked it in fiscal 2024 (CR029). The Commission's closure date and the termination date come from search snippets only (not recorded as a source row).

Three things follow for the thesis. First, regulators treated web-native collaborative tools as a source of innovation pressure on the suite incumbent, which is exactly the "AI lowered entry barriers" logic in the supervisor's framing, one technology generation earlier. Second, S7 (acquiring AI-native companies) is constrained by competition law when the target is a leading or potential competitor; the Adobe/Figma outcome is the precedent. The case study files do show later acquisitions (Canva, Superhuman/Grammarly with Coda and Rows), which are smaller and not obviously vulnerable. A short commentary exists (CR024) and gives the legal reading, but it is metadata only. Third, the decisions define markets in a way that is already out of date for the agentic era: nothing in them contemplates an external agent as a user of the tool. The student should cite the primary documents and then state explicitly that market definitions based on professional users of a tool may not describe markets in which agents produce the artifact.

### 2.4 Generative AI and creative work: what the empirical studies show

The peer-reviewed evidence converges on five points.

1. **Productivity and quality gains, larger for weaker performers.** Noy and Zhang (2023) report 40% less time and 18% higher quality in professional writing tasks, with inequality between workers reduced; Brynjolfsson et al. (2025) find +15% productivity in customer support with the largest gains for less experienced workers; Zhou and Lee (2024) find that text-to-image AI raised creative productivity by 25% and the value of output (favorites per view) by 50% on an art-sharing platform. Doshi and Hauser (2024) find that AI ideas make stories more creative, especially for less creative writers.
2. **Homogenisation.** Doshi and Hauser (2024) also find that AI-enabled stories are more similar to each other. For tool strategy this is double-edged: it favours tools that supply differentiators (brand kits, design systems, proprietary templates; S4) over tools that only generate.
3. **Substitution in freelance creative labor.** Hui et al. (2024) find reductions in employment and earnings for freelancers in highly affected occupations after ChatGPT, DALL-E 2 and Midjourney; Demirci et al. (2025) study the same marketplace setting (V-meta only; the heterogeneity by task type is from memory and must be checked). Eloundou et al. (2024) give task-exposure estimates (V-meta only, headline results from memory).
4. **Professionals see AI as assistive, juniors are at risk.** Interviews with 20 UX designers (Li et al., 2024) report confidence in originality and an assistive role for AI among experienced designers and risks of skill degradation for juniors; a smaller interview study of 19 UI/UX designers (Khan et al., 2025, preprint) finds AI as a partner in divergent thinking. Jiang et al. (2023) give the artists' side (consent, competition).
5. **The frontier is jagged.** Dell'Acqua et al. (2023, V-meta only) is relevant when asking when an agent-made artifact can be trusted.

What is contested or missing. Almost all studies use writing, illustration or ideation tasks; none, in what I found, studies the production of *business artifacts* (decks, brand-compliant social assets, interface prototypes) by non-designers using AI-native tools, which is the Canva, Gamma and Figma Make use case. None studies agents that operate inside a SaaS product, or the consumption of AI credits by tool users. Vendor-run surveys partly fill the void: Figma's 2025 AI report samples 2,500 product builders in seven countries (CR034, landing page only) and press coverage suggests that developers are more positive than designers about AI quality gains (CR035). Anthropic's Economic Index (CR036, snippet) finds 57% augmentation and 43% automation overall, with arts and media over-represented relative to their workforce share. These are practitioner sources with unknown sampling and should be used as indications. The systematic reviews already in the registers (Sharma and Singhal, 2026, NEW16) and the CST reviews (Cox et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025) support the same conclusion: the research agenda is about human-AI co-creation in the individual task, not about the industry that supplies the tools.

Hwang (2022) is a useful baseline: AI-empowered co-creative tools, before the 2022 to 2023 boom, "mostly support the generation and execution of ideas and are less involved in the early stages of co-creation" (CR005). The agentic tools of 2025 to 2026 (Figma Make, Gamma's agent, Adobe Express AI Assistant, Claude Design, Google Pics; see grey register G26, G28 and the case files) move precisely into that gap, from execution towards upstream ideation and orchestration. That is a testable claim about how the *role* of the tool changes (SQ1), and the CST literature has not yet caught up.

### 2.5 The AI and creative-tools market, 2023 to 2026: commoditization of artifact production

The registers already hold the news trail for the market (Claude Design G26, Google Pics G28 and G29, Gamma G30 and G31, Lovable G32, Base44 G34, Microsoft Office Agent G36). I add four observations from primary documents.

**The incumbents buy the same model layer.** In April 2025 Figma, Adobe and Canva each integrated OpenAI's gpt-image-1 model (CR035, press). Adobe also pairs this with its own Firefly models and "an expansive partner model ecosystem" (CR029). If the model layer is shared, differentiation moves to workflow, context and distribution: this is S4 and S6 in the thesis taxonomy.

**The cost of AI is already a business-model variable.** Figma's 2025 10-K reports AI credits introduced across all seats in 2025 with enforcement from March 2026 and optional pay-as-you-go credit plans, flags margin pressure from AI-related features, and warns that customers may demand "options that change who bears the cost of AI usage ... including by allowing customers to use their own third-party AI credentials or accounts (for example, bring-your-own-key arrangements)" (CR031). The same filing says Figma added its own MCP server to Dev Mode so that an agent in a code editor can read Figma designs (CR031). These two passages are direct primary evidence for S2 and S5 and for SQ3, and are among the strongest items in this stream. They agree with the case files for Adobe Express (credits since 13 September 2023; Premium at USD 9.99 with 250 generative credits per the case file's snippet-level price source) and Gamma (credits per slide and image; "unlimited" with fair-use footnote from September 2026, per the case file).

**Scale of the incumbents.** Adobe's 10-K lists "AI or cloud-native companies" among its competitors, reports Digital Media ARR growth of 11.5%, and positions Firefly and GenStudio as an end-to-end ideation-to-creation platform (CR029). The SaaSpocalypse literature (stream on L1) argues for structural pressure; this filing shows no evidence of a collapse in the creative segment in fiscal 2025, but the filing covers the year to November 2025 and ARR is a lagging measure. Figma's TAM claim (CR030) shows the opposite logic: a bigger market if more people participate in design. The evidence is consistent with both "AI widens the population" and "AI moves value to agents", which the thesis must distinguish.

**Commoditization of artifact production is asserted more than measured.** I found no academic study that measures price per artifact, entry rates in creative software, or concentration after 2023. The registers hold two preprints on builder saturation and solo entrepreneurship (NEW06, NEW08). The claim that artifact production is commoditised therefore rests on practitioner sources, and on indirect evidence such as Hui et al. (2024). I could not retrieve any serious analysis of "design is code" or "prompt to app" beyond vendor material.

### 2.6 Legal and governance aspects as a strategic lever (rival hypothesis R-governance)

The question here is whether governance (training data, indemnity, provenance, authorship tools) is a source of advantage or a cost of doing business.

*Evidence for governance as lever.* Adobe markets Firefly as "commercially safe" and trained on "data Adobe has the rights to use", attaches Content Credentials to every Firefly output and offers IP indemnification with "certain of our enterprise and teams solutions" (CR029; announced in June 2023, CR039 and CR040, where the indemnity was explicitly aimed at enterprise fears). Canva announced in October 2023 a USD 200 million, three-year fund for creators who opt in to AI training, with marketplace works not used by default (CR047). Grammarly Authorship turns a governance concern (who wrote this) into a product: beta in Google Docs in October 2024, in Word in April 2025, in Canvas in September 2025, and "over a million" reports in under a year (CR046, vendor claim). Liang et al. (2023, V-meta; finding from memory) report that AI-text detectors are biased against non-native English writers, which would make a process-based record a rational alternative to detection. The three incumbents have made different governance choices (licensed data and indemnity; creator remuneration; process transparency), which is exactly the variation a case study needs.

*Evidence that governance is also a cost and a risk.* Figma suspended Make Design in July 2024 after it reproduced Apple's Weather app; its CEO stated that the feature was not trained on Figma content, community files or app designs and blamed commissioned design systems and QA (CR048). Adobe faced a user backlash in June 2024 over terms letting it access content "through both manual and automated methods", and clarified that it does not train generative AI on user content except for Adobe Stock contributions (CR049). These are episodes where trust itself was at stake, and governance acted as a constraint on strategy.

*Law is moving in ways that cut both ways.* The U.S. Copyright Office's pre-publication Part 3 report says that training on a large, diverse dataset "will often be transformative" while leaving room for the market-dilution harm (CR037). In the UK, the *Getty v Stability AI* High Court judgment (4 November 2025, summarised by law firms; CR042, snippet) rejected the secondary infringement claim and upheld only narrow trade mark points; Stability obtained leave to appeal. The *Bartz v Anthropic* settlement shows that the way data were acquired (pirated books) carried the liability (CR043, snippet; final approval reported by only one source). The EU Article 50 transparency obligations apply from 2 August 2026, with a code of practice finalised on 10 June 2026 (CR044, snippet). C2PA Content Credentials are an opt-in, cryptographically bound provenance standard and explicitly make no judgment about whether content is good (CR041). Reading these together: litigation outcomes so far do not make training on unlicensed data uniformly unlawful, which weakens the "commercially safe" premium as a durable moat; transparency regulation may make provenance a baseline expectation, which would turn it from a differentiator into a compliance cost. Both outcomes are compatible with R-governance as a *transitional* lever, which is a more defensible version of the rival hypothesis than "governance as lasting advantage".

Appraisal note. Most legal items here are law-firm summaries and press; the primary documents (USCO report, C2PA paper, Adobe 10-K) are the only ones I read. The student must open the judgment (Getty) and the Commission code text.

### 2.7 Agentic AI and the "who is the user" problem

No source in this stream addresses agents as users of creation tools in a scholarly way. The CMA and Commission documents speak of professional human designers; the CST literature measures human experience; the empirical studies of AI at work (Noy and Zhang, Brynjolfsson et al.) put the AI inside a human's workflow. The only direct evidence of tools being offered to external agents is in company disclosures: Figma's MCP server (CR031) and the case files (Adobe in ChatGPT, Copilot and Claude; Canva and Gamma APIs and apps; Grammarly agents inside Superhuman). The scholarly literature on agentic AI and platform governance is already in the registers (NEW01 on boundary resources, NEW02 and NEW03 on headless firms and accountability boundaries, NEW05 on superplatforms attacking agents). What this stream adds is that the creative-tool literature has not yet been connected to that one.

## 4. Implications for the thesis

**Propositions the literature supports.** (P-a) AI raises output and quality most for less skilled users (Noy and Zhang; Brynjolfsson et al.; Doshi and Hauser; Zhou and Lee), which supports the premise that the population of artifact producers expands (L1). (P-b) Homogenisation of AI output (Doshi and Hauser) supports S4: proprietary context and brand-specific assets become the differentiator. (P-c) Competition authorities have documented that web-native collaborative design tools create real competitive pressure on suite incumbents (CR025 to CR027), the pre-AI precedent for the thesis's "entry barrier" story. (P-d) Incumbents themselves name the cost of AI and BYOK as open pricing questions (CR031), supporting S5 and SQ3.

**Propositions it challenges or qualifies.** (Q-a) Substitution is not uniform: Hui et al. (2024) find losses for freelancers, Li et al. (2024) find seniors confident; so "creation work disappears" is too strong. (Q-b) "Governance as lever" is a transitional lever at best: courts and regulators are not converging on unlicensed training as unlawful, and transparency rules may make provenance a baseline. (Q-c) Analyst taxonomies do not treat creation tools as a single category, so claims such as "productivity SaaS is threatened" need a precise population definition.

**Strategies informed.** S1 (own agent): Hwang's mapping of AI tools to execution stages, and the shift to agentic design tools. S2 (used by external agents): only Figma's MCP server is documented in a filing I read. S4: homogenisation and brand differentiation. S5: Figma credits and BYOK; freemium theory. S6: regulator market definitions. S7: Adobe/Figma as the constraint. R-governance: Adobe, Canva, Grammarly and Figma episodes. R-outcome: no support found.

**Gaps the thesis can claim (RQ-L4).**
1. No scholarly analysis, found in this search, of how creation-oriented SaaS (design, presentation, writing) responds to agentic AI at the business model level; the CST and HCI literatures study the individual user, the competition filings study the pre-AI market.
2. No academic treatment found of Adobe's subscription transition, Canva's marketplace model, or AI credit pricing in creative tools; case-based work on these is feasible with public filings (Adobe and Figma 10-K, Canva press) as this review shows.
3. No study of tools used by agents rather than humans; the market definitions in the Adobe/Figma decisions assume human professional users.
4. Governance has been studied as law and ethics, not as strategy; the five cases show variation in governance choices that has not been compared.
