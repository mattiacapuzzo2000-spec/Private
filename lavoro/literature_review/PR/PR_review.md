## 2. Thematic synthesis

### 2.1 The economic premise: information goods, near-zero marginal cost, and what inference changes

The classic economics of information goods rests on high first-copy cost and negligible reproduction cost (Shapiro & Varian, 1999; Goldfarb & Tucker, 2019). Three pricing consequences follow and are well established. First, cost-based pricing is meaningless when marginal cost is close to zero, so value-based and versioned pricing dominate in theory (Shapiro & Varian, 1999). Second, bundling many goods is profitable because aggregation makes valuations predictable, but Bakos and Brynjolfsson state the condition explicitly: economies of aggregation arise "if their marginal costs are very low" (Bakos & Brynjolfsson, 2000; see also 1999, who note that the results do not extend to most physical goods). Third, a fixed fee beside a usage-based schedule is profit-improving whenever metering is costly (Sundararajan, 2004), and many consumers prefer a flat rate even when it costs more (Lambrecht & Skiera, 2006). These results explain the "development-based, highly scalable" cost structure that the case files attribute to the five firms before 2023 (high fixed R&D, low variable cost per user), the course idea that variable costs reduce scalability, and the persistence of the seat.

What is new is that each AI use now carries a computation cost. Casado and Bornstein (2020) were the first widely read investor argument: AI companies "look more like services", with gross margins "often in the 50-60% range" against 60% to 80%+ for SaaS, because of cloud infrastructure and human support (a16z, 2020). That essay is anecdotal and about pre-LLM machine learning, but its mechanism (cost scaling with use) is the one now visible at Figma. Bessemer's 2025 benchmarks add AI-native archetypes with about 25% average gross margin ("Supernovas", often negative) and about 60% ("Shooting Stars") (Bessemer Venture Partners, 2025). ICONIQ's surveys put average gross margin on AI products at 45% in 2025, with respondents projecting 53% in 2026 and 59% in 2027 (ICONIQ Growth, 2026b), and Poyar's 2026 survey reports a median target AI margin of about 50%, with only 12% of companies aiming at 80% or more (Poyar, 2026). The debate is therefore not whether AI margins are lower than SaaS margins (all sources agree they are) but whether the gap is structural or transitional. Peer-reviewed evidence on this point is absent; the best quantitative evidence is a preprint showing that the price for a fixed benchmark performance falls 5x to 10x per year while the price of running frontier models rises 3x to 18x per year (Gundlach et al., 2025), and an Epoch AI analysis showing declines of 9x to 900x per year depending on the milestone (Cottier et al., 2025). The reconciliation matters for the thesis: cost per unit of a fixed capability falls fast, but vendors keep moving users to frontier and agentic capabilities, so cost per user can rise. BCG adds the pricing implication: cost-plus pricing "undermines predictability" and, as model costs fall, "revenues may erode in parallel unless usage grows exponentially" (BCG, 2025).

*Established:* the AI cost is real, variable and heavy-tailed (BCG reports margin variance above 70 percentage points across accounts of one vendor; Poyar reports from experience that the top 10% of users drive more than 70% of token consumption). *Contested:* whether margins converge to SaaS levels. *Missing:* any peer-reviewed measurement of cost-to-serve for application-layer SaaS firms.

### 2.2 SaaS and software pricing research: what the academic base says

The academic literature on software and SaaS pricing is pre-generative-AI. Lehmann and Buxmann (2009) give the vocabulary (assessment base, payment structure, bundling); Saltan and Smolander (2021) show in a multivocal review of 387 items that SaaS pricing is under-managed; Ma and Seidmann (2015) model SaaS with per-transaction charges against packaged software. On usage-based versus selling, Balasubramanian, Bhattacharya and Krishnan (2015) show that pay-per-use beats selling in a monopoly only if the psychological "ticking meter" cost is low, while in a duopoly selling can yield higher profit. Combined with the flat-rate bias (Lambrecht & Skiera, 2006) and the pain-of-paying argument (Prelec & Loewenstein, 1998), this predicts what the cases show: in competitive markets (Canva, Adobe Express, Figma, Gamma all face close rivals) the flat component (the seat) stays and metering enters as allowance, cap or add-on. Grubb and Osborne (2015) add that allowances plus alerts are a stable tariff design in which consumers are inattentive and overconfident, which matches Figma's usage dashboard, add-ons and spend limits.

The freemium literature gives the second building block. Dominant firms can rationally give away good products when network externalities are present (Shi, Zhang & Srinivasan, 2019); feature-limited freemium can beat charging for everything (Niculescu & Wu, 2014); an additional premium version can raise sales of the existing premium version (Gu, Kannan & Ma, 2018); and firms should vary the free allowance dynamically (Lambrecht & Misra, 2017). Conversion depends on perceived premium fit (Wagner, Benlian & Hess, 2014). None of these models has a free-user cost that grows with usage. That is precisely what changed in 2025 and 2026, and it is the gap an S5 argument can claim: freemium theory assumes a free tier is nearly free to serve.

For value-based versus cost-based logic, Hinterhuber (2008) documents that more than 80% of firms price mainly on cost or competition despite the value-based ideal. This base rate matters because the AI credit systems in the cases mirror inference cost (credits per generation or per agent run), consistent with the cost-based default, and practitioner sources say so openly: credits are "a bridge solution" adopted because "costs are known and value is not" (Metronome, 2025).

*Established:* theory supports hybrid fixed fee plus metered component, and free tiers under network effects. *Contested:* none directly, since no peer-reviewed study tests these models on AI. *Missing:* empirical tests with generative-AI cost structures; the closest are Gupta's coding of AI-company pricing pages (preprint, 2026) and the analytical models already in the register (Mahmood, 2024; Ning et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Podolski, 2026).

### 2.3 What practitioners observe: hybrid layering over seats

Every practitioner source in the stream reports the same modal pattern. In Poyar's surveys hybrid pricing rose from 27% to 41% (2025) and stood at 37% in the 2026 survey, with flat-fee and seat-based falling (21% to 15% for seat-based in 2025); the 2026 survey notes that the largest software companies now change pricing most (Poyar, 2025; 2026). ICONIQ's builders report 38% hybrid, 36% subscription or seat, 19% usage, 6% outcome in April 2025 (via SaaStr; ICONIQ Growth, 2025), and by Q2 2026 consumption pricing is 42% and outcome-based 23%, with firms blending 1.7 models on average (ICONIQ Growth, 2026b). Bain's analysis of more than 30 incumbent SaaS vendors found about 35% simply raised seat prices and about 65% layered an AI meter on seats (Adobe and Salesforce named), and none had moved entirely to usage or outcome pricing (Bain, 2025). A year later Bain found that about one in five AI-native vendors still relies mainly on seats, that "most usage-based pricing is capacity-based pricing with overages", and that about four in five vendors choose capacity models, naming Adobe Firefly credits (Bain, 2026). Simon-Kucher reports that for AI agents today seat-based pricing is the largest category (41%), then fixed fee (30%), usage (23%) and outcome (7%) (Simon-Kucher, 2026). Metronome's catalogue of more than 50 AI pricing models finds hybrid to be the norm and distinguishes three functions of credits: compute proxies, abstracted value bundles and access gating (Metronome, 2026).

Two caveats matter. First, nearly all these surveys are drawn from AI builders, investors' portfolios or newsletter readers, and several are run by billing or consulting firms with a commercial interest in pricing change (Metronome sponsors Growth Unhinged; Simon-Kucher and BCG sell pricing work). Second, categories are not comparable: Bain notes that some "outcome-based" pricing is actually tied to workflow outputs (Bain, 2026). Buyer-side evidence is a useful counterweight: Futurum's 2H 2026 survey finds buyers rank per-seat last for core software (12.6%) but first for AI functionality billed separately (42.3%), because they want budget predictability (Futurum Research, 2026). The "seats are dead" narrative is therefore contested; the evidence supports "seats persist as the anchor and entitlement, consumption is layered on".

### 2.4 Credits as a pricing device: cost control or value capture?

Credits are the central S5 mechanism. The sources agree on four functions. (1) Cost cap: credits normalise volatile LLM usage into a prepaid, capped quantity (Metronome, 2025); a16z says well-designed credits "protect your gross margins" because each AI user generates inference cost, unlike a traditional SaaS user (Erten & Wang, 2026); Poyar lists "margins. By capping usage" among four reasons hybrid is popular (Poyar, 2025). (2) Predictability for the buyer (Futurum Research, 2026; Bain, 2026). (3) Upsell path: Adobe management describes agent monetization as "basically about credit consumption" with upsell (case AE-23); Figma sells add-ons and pay-as-you-go beyond the seat credits (F23). (4) Abstraction: a credit hides tokens, but a16z warns that "a credit is a currency, not a value metric. What matters is what the credit buys" (Erten & Wang, 2026), and Metronome's interviewees concede that "customers don't know what a credit does" (Metronome, 2025).

The analytical question for the thesis is whether the credit is cost control or value capture. Three tests separate them. *Test A, what does the credit unit track?* A cost-control credit maps to compute (credits per generation, credits per agent run), a value-capture credit to a recognizable unit of work. Metronome calls the first "compute proxies"; a16z's survey (n = 50) found 27 buyers preferring credits tied to recognizable work against 14 preferring tokens. *Test B, do credit rates move with cost?* If credits are cost-plus, rate cards should follow model costs. *Test C, is the credit allowance tied to the seat or to the work?* An allowance per seat protects the seat anchor; a pooled balance moves toward usage. Section 3 applies these tests to the cases.

The comparison with other incumbents is consistent: Microsoft sells Copilot Credits as a usage model that "complements fixed subscription licensing" with admin cost controls (Microsoft, 2026); Salesforce runs a per-conversation price ($2), Flex Credits (20 credits per action, implying about $0.10) and a $5 per user licence in parallel (Salesforce, 2026); Notion sells per-member Standard and Select credits that cannot be pooled, plus purchasable workspace credits (Notion, 2026). Cursor's June 2025 move from a 500-request allotment to $20 of usage at API rates shows both the cost logic and the backlash risk when allowances are tightened (Zeff, 2025). Gupta's coding of AI-native firms finds subscription with usage quota to be the dominant observable model (55% of observable rows) (Gupta, 2026).

*Established:* credits are the dominant AI-metering device, and practitioners describe them as cost-based. *Contested:* whether credits are a durable value metric or "a lifeline, just not the AI pricing endgame" (Poyar, 2026). *Missing:* any study of how credit rates are adjusted over time; no academic work on credits as a pricing instrument.

### 2.5 Rationing free AI

Free AI rationing has no direct academic study. The theory of free tiers (Section 2.2) says free offers pay off when they build network effects or conversion; Lambrecht and Misra (2017) imply that the free allowance should respond to demand conditions. With AI, the cost of the free allowance enters the objective. Practitioner sources confirm that vendors behave this way: Poyar's data show that the biggest SaaS companies, including Figma and Canva, made large AI pricing changes in the first months of 2026 (Poyar, 2026), and BCG recommends "seeding" limited access to new agentic capabilities in the base product before monetizing (BCG, 2025). The cases (Section 3) provide the sharpest evidence.

### 2.6 Outcome-based and value-based pricing: the rival hypothesis

**Academic base.** Outcome-based contracting is studied mainly in servitization. Hünerberg and Hüttmann (2003) and Bonnemeier, Burianek and Reichwald (2010) set out performance- and result-based revenue models; Hou and Neely (2018) analyse provider risks; Sjödin et al. (2020) show in a multiple-case study that shifting to outcome-based business models is a full business model innovation that requires co-defining value, designing provision and delivering value in use with the customer, and that four of six cases failed. The literature agrees on conditions: measurable performance, risk transfer the provider can bear, trust and shared data. None of it concerns software or generative AI. Ulaga and Reinartz (2011) and Visnjic et al. (2016) are background on hybrid offerings.

**Practitioner claims.** Advocacy is strong and comes largely from vendors and consultants: Bain's 2025 technology report (G01, in the register) urges to "price for outcomes, not log-ons"; BCG advises moving "from traditional seat-based licensing to outcome-oriented pricing" while managing seat compression (BCG, 2025); Taylor argues outcome-based pricing is "the future of software business models" (Sierra, 2026); IDC projects consumption or outcome pricing of enterprise applications to rise from 13% to 63% over ten years (register G06). Adoption evidence tells another story. Outcome pricing exists in customer support, where a resolved conversation is countable: Zendesk (August 2024), Intercom Fin at $0.99 per outcome, HubSpot Breeze Customer Agent at $0.50 per resolved conversation from 14 April 2026, Sierra, Decagon (Zendesk, 2024; Intercom, 2026; HubSpot, 2026; Bain, 2026). Elsewhere it is rare: 5% primary model in Poyar's 2025 survey (with 25% expecting it by 2028, per a secondary summary), 6% in ICONIQ's April 2025 survey rising to 18% and 23% in later waves, 7% for AI agents in Simon-Kucher's study, about 10% of hybrid AI meters in Bain's June 2026 pricing analysis, and 2% of incumbents in a McKinsey analysis reported second-hand (unverified). The intention-adoption gap is visible within one source: 35% of Simon-Kucher's respondents plan outcome-based add-ons while 7% of agents are priced that way.

**Why it stays narrow.** Bain states three conditions: the outcome must be observable, uniquely attributable to the AI, and contractible; "most workflows" fail this (Bain, 2026). BCG relays a16z survey barriers: 47% of buyers cannot define outcomes, 36% worry about predictability, 25% about attribution, 24% about factors outside the vendor's control (BCG, 2025). The most-cited outcome products also blur the definition: Intercom counts an "assumed resolution" when the customer leaves without asking again (Intercom, 2026); HubSpot itself says "most AI tools aren't priced on outcomes because they can't promise consistent results" and pays through a credit ledger (HubSpot, 2026), so outcome pricing and credits merge. Futurum's buyer panel finds that vendors with pure outcome pricing do not show stronger spending momentum than hybrid vendors (Futurum Research, 2026). Sjödin et al. predict this: outcome pricing is a business model change, not a price tag.

**Output versus outcome versus effort.** Bain's typology is the most useful device for the thesis: effort-based meters charge for resources consumed (tokens, compute, agent hours), output-based meters for what the AI produces (summaries, drafts, generations), outcome-based meters for a business result; "the simplest test is who carries the quality risk" (Bain, 2026). Creation tools sell outputs (a design, a deck, a document) whose value to the user is subjective and realised downstream. They cannot satisfy the three conditions, which predicts that the rival hypothesis will not appear in creation SaaS even if it spreads in support and sales.

### 2.7 Who pays when an external agent does the work?

No source in the stream treats this question directly; it is a gap. Three building blocks exist. First, cost incidence: when a user's ChatGPT or Claude drives a vendor's tool, the host model provider bears the inference of the agent loop, and the vendor bears only its own tool-side compute (rendering, generation, storage). Kapoor et al. (2024) show agent runs are costly and variable, which is why vendors meter tool calls that trigger their own models. Second, seat leakage: Figma itself warns (S2, S10 in the case file) that agents "could reduce the number of paid seats required for a given level of output" (S2 and S10 in the Figma case file), and BCG reports 40% of buyers cite seat reduction as the main lever to cut software spend (BCG, 2025). Third, the layer principle: a16z advises to "price at the highest layer of value that [a firm] can reliably measure, attribute, and defend" and, for tool vendors, to meter the work done rather than tokens (Erten & Wang, 2026). Gamma's changelog states that its agent endpoints are "priced by the work the agent does" (Gamma, 2026), in Bain's terms an effort or output meter.

### 2.8 Convergence and contradictions

The sources converge on: hybrid seat-plus-consumption as the interim equilibrium; credits as the dominant device and mainly cost-driven; outcome pricing narrow and conditional on observability; AI margins below SaaS. They conflict on: the speed of seat decline (Poyar, vendors and BCG versus Bain and Futurum buyers); whether credits are transitional; and whether margins will converge (ICONIQ respondents expect 59% by 2027; Gundlach et al. show frontier prices rising). Peer-reviewed work supplies the mechanisms (hybrid tariffs, freemium, flat-rate bias, outcome contracting conditions); grey literature supplies the observations. Peer-reviewed evidence on AI-era pricing remains thin; the preprint by Gupta (2026) is the only structured empirical coding found.

## 3. Anchoring to the cases

Figures below are taken from `lavoro/casi/*.md`; source-level labels are those of the case files.

**Figma (strongest evidence for S5 as cost-driven).** Non-GAAP gross margin fell from 92% (FY2024) to 88% (FY2025) and 82% (Q3 2026); GAAP from 91% (2023) to 80% (Q3 2026). In Q3 2026 non-GAAP cost of revenue was USD 70.3 million against USD 37.3 million a year earlier (+88%) while revenue grew from USD 274.2 million to USD 391.4 million (+43%); the share of revenue consumed by non-GAAP cost of revenue rose from about 7.9% (Q3 2024: 15.6 of 198.6) to about 18.0% (Q3 2026). The 10-Q attributes USD 90.3 million of the nine-month rise to AI infrastructure. Response: seat anchored (Full seat USD 16 Professional, 55 Organization, 90 Enterprise), every seat given a capped monthly credit allotment (Full 3,000 / 3,500 / 4,250; Dev, Collab, View, Starter 500; Starter and View also 150 per day), enforcement from 18 March 2026, add-ons from 11 March 2026 with pay-as-you-go, admin per-user limits, agent consumption of credits from 6 October 2026 (300+ credits for a five-screen flow), and in August 2026 add-ons rose to 2x credits at the same price on Professional and 1.6x on Organization and Enterprise, citing "token efficiency".
- *Theory fit.* The seat plus allowance is the three-part-tariff design predicted by Sundararajan (2004) and by the flat-rate bias (Lambrecht & Skiera, 2006). The mechanism is the one Bakos and Brynjolfsson make conditional on low marginal cost: the margin trend shows the condition failing. Test A: credit units follow compute (per prompt, per agent run). Test B: the August 2026 rate move confirms cost tracking: when model cost fell, credits per dollar rose (the BCG warning that cost-plus revenue erodes, here deliberately conceded to defend seat ARPU). Test C: allowances attach to the seat, so the seat anchor survives. Figma also reports that over 75% of Org and Enterprise users who exceeded limits kept using credits and Pro teams buying add-ons had more than 3x average ARR, which is evidence that rationing did not destroy engagement (company data, level A in the case file). On external agents, access is tied to the seat; writing to the canvas was "free during the beta" with a usage-based price announced, and generation tools (`generate_image`, Slides in ChatGPT) consume credits: the incidence rule is "metered where Figma's own compute is used".
- *Gap.* Theory does not say how long a vendor can keep read access to the canvas free when agents replace human seats (Figma warns about this in its own filings).

**Canva (cost shock plus mass freemium).** Free AI went from "up to 200 Standard or 20 Premium AI uses" to "up to 20 Standard or Premium AI uses" (9 September 2026), then to "limited access" wording on the pricing page (30 October to 2 November 2026). The Q2 2026 update cut the 2026 growth target from 30% to 20% as "a deliberate decision to get the economics right", with "average cost of serving an AI task ... too high" and "relying too heavily on frontier models"; cost per AI task was cut by about 90%. Pro annual price moved US$144 to US$180 (late July to early August 2026) and back to US$144 on 11 September 2026; the Business plan is US$200 per person per year against Teams US$100; AI Pass is US$100 per person per month (help page snippet). Over 265m MAU and over 31m paying at end 2025 implies roughly 12% paid, so free AI cost scales with the 88%.
- *Theory fit.* Freemium models (Shi et al., 2019; Niculescu & Wu, 2014) justify a free tier under network effects but assume near-zero free-user cost; Canva is the empirical counterexample, and Lambrecht and Misra's (2017) dynamic free allowance is the closest fit. Cost-based logic dominates Test B (quota and price follow cost) while price reversals (US$180 to US$144) show limited pricing power, as Hinterhuber's cost-or-competition default and Poyar's remark that commodity-like AI units give little pricing power would predict. The progressive concealment of the free quota is consistent with shrouding (Gabaix & Laibson, 2005) but this is the thesis's inference, not a tested finding.

**Adobe Express (cost pass-through at the parent, funnel at the product).** Generative credits since 13 September 2023 with monthly credits for free users; Creative Cloud prices rose from November 2023 "given new costs associated with producing generative AI content" while Express plans were unchanged (Adobe, 2023: verified); Premium US$9.99 per month with 250 credits (page snippet), a seat price unchanged since 2021; AI Assistant promised at GA through Firefly credits and packs; Q2 FY26 management said agent monetization is "basically about credit consumption" and rebalanced toward friction-free freemium "at the cost of short-term ARR"; creative freemium MAU above 100 million (+70%) in Q3 FY26.
- *Theory fit.* Bain classifies Adobe as a hybrid (meter on seat) and Firefly credits as a capacity model (Bain, 2025; 2026). Express is cross-subsidised by the parent, so rationing is lighter and Shi et al.'s dominant-firm logic (free as strategy) fits. Adobe meters generation (output), not entry: free entry for guests in ChatGPT, Claude, Slack and Gemini, with sign-in unlocking generative capabilities. This is the output meter in Bain's typology and the "who pays" rule: the vendor's own generation draws its credit ledger.

**Grammarly (prompt caps on a stable seat price).** GrammarlyGO (March 2023) launched with 100 prompts for Free and 500 for Premium; Pro (September 2024) has 2,000 prompts and costs US$12 per member per month on annual billing, unchanged; Business US$33 (annual) in October 2025; Go 1.0 (12 August 2026) adds "soft usage limits" and the stated expectation of "usage-based pricing aligned with the value agents create". Outcome measurement exists (Effective Communication Score, ROI Report, October 2024) but is not a pricing meter. 
- *Theory fit.* This is the clearest instance of measurement without contracting: Grammarly can measure outcomes but charges per seat, which supports Bain's three conditions (observable yes, attributable and contractible no) and the flat-rate bias. Prompt caps are the earliest and simplest allowance form.

**Gamma (AI-native benchmark).** Credits-based pricing since 15 September 2025; Free 400 credits without refresh, Plus 1,500, Pro 4,000, Ultra 20,000 monthly; "Unlimited AI creations" on the pricing page in September 2026 (contradicted by the help center, unresolved in the case file); agent endpoints "priced by the work the agent does" (5 October 2026); MCP generations charge credits while reading, searching and exporting do not; API keys on every plan from 22 October 2026. Self-reported: USD 100 million ARR, profitable, about 50 staff.
- *Theory fit.* Gamma designs credits from the start (Test A: metered per slide and image, now per agent work), so it is an effort/output meter rather than an outcome meter. The "unlimited" marketing next to credits fits the ticking-meter argument (Balasubramanian et al., 2015). It is profitable on self-report despite per-use cost, consistent with the possibility that AI-native firms accept lower gross margin for growth (Bessemer, 2025) or that fixed-cost leverage with 50 staff compensates; nothing in the sources allows a firm conclusion.

**Cross-case result for the rival.** None of the five cases uses outcome-based pricing. The literature explains why (three conditions; creation outputs are subjective and downstream), and it also warns that "outcome" labels may be applied loosely, so the coding in the case files should record the meter type (effort, output, outcome) explicitly. Canva Grow (performance advertising) is the only potential candidate and is bundled in plans.

## 4. Implications for the thesis

**Testable propositions** (to be stated in the framework chapter, each with the evidence status).

- **P1 (cost-cap hybrid).** When AI adds a variable and heavy-tailed cost per use to a formerly near-zero-marginal-cost product, incumbents keep the seat as anchor and add capped allowances rather than switching the metric. *Support:* all five cases, Bain (2025; 2026), Poyar, ICONIQ, Gupta. *Risk:* selection bias in surveys of AI builders.
- **P2 (cost-based, not value-based, logic in credit design).** Credit units and quotas track the vendor's inference cost and are adjusted when costs change; value capture appears separately through price increases and tiering (Adobe 2023; Canva Business; Figma Full seat). *Support:* Figma August 2026 (2x credits at the same price), Canva 90% cost per task cut and quota cuts, Metronome, a16z. *Challenge:* none found; Hinterhuber's base rate (80% cost or competition pricing) makes it the expected outcome.
- **P3 (free-tier rationing scales with free base and lack of cross-subsidy).** Firms with large free bases and no parent cross-subsidy ration free AI more and sooner (Canva) than firms where freemium feeds a larger suite (Adobe) or where the product is AI-native with a credit model from the start (Gamma). *Support:* case ordering; theory (Lambrecht & Misra, 2017) is compatible. *Needs:* quantification of free AI cost, not available.
- **P4 (meter location).** Application-layer vendors meter their own generation or agent work (output or effort), not access and not outcomes, in line with Bain's typology and a16z's layer principle. *Support:* Adobe, Gamma, Figma. 
- **P5 (incidence for external agents).** When an external agent uses the product, the vendor charges where its own compute or protected assets are used (generation tools metered, read access bundled with the seat or free), and anchors access to the seat to protect seat revenue. *Support:* Figma, Adobe, Gamma. *Gap:* no literature; cases are the evidence, which is where the thesis can contribute.
- **P6 (scalability erosion).** In development-based software the share of revenue consumed by cost of revenue is stable; with AI it rises with usage. *Support:* Figma (about 7.9% to about 18.0% of revenue, non-GAAP), ICONIQ and Poyar margin data. 
- **P7 (rival: outcome pricing).** Outcome-based pricing appears only where outcome is observable, attributable and contractible; creation SaaS fails these conditions; absence in all five cases is therefore predicted, not surprising. *Support:* Bain (2026); Sjödin et al. (2020); no counter-case found. *Falsifier to watch:* Canva Grow or an agent product that sells finished campaigns.
- **P8 (seat persistence is buyer-driven).** Buyers want seat predictability for AI add-ons (Futurum Research, 2026), reinforcing P1. *Challenged by:* vendor surveys that report seat decline.

**Strategies informed.** S5 is the best-documented strategy in the stream. S2 (external agents) is informed only on terms: the cost-incidence rule (P5) and the seat-leakage risk. S1 (own agent): effort metering of agent runs. S4 (proprietary context) is not covered here. S6 and S7 (scope, acquisitions) link through bundling theory (Bakos & Brynjolfsson) and through credit systems that span acquired tools (Figma Weave credits, Canva with Leonardo). R-outcome: the stream supports its marginal role.

**Gaps the thesis can claim (L4).** (1) No peer-reviewed study of AI-era pricing at incumbent creation SaaS; only preprints and analytical models. (2) No study of credits as an instrument (cost cap versus value metric) or of how credit rates are adjusted over time. (3) No study of who pays when external agents use SaaS tools. (4) Free-tier theory lacks a variable free-user cost. (5) Survey evidence is from AI builders, not from creation-oriented incumbents. (6) "Outcome-based" is used inconsistently; a coding by meter type is missing. The thesis can contribute a longitudinal, dated, multi-case record of meter changes (Figma, Canva, Adobe Express, Grammarly, Gamma) with exact quotas and cost data.

**Cautions.** Many numbers come from vendor-affiliated or investor sources; Poyar's and Metronome's content is sponsored by a billing platform; Simon-Kucher and BCG sell pricing services; ICONIQ 2025 was read only through a summary; McKinsey figures are second-hand.
